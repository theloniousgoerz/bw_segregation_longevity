\section{Abstract}
% =====================================================================


Racial segregation is widely understood to advantage White Americans at the expense of Black Americans through differing neighborhood environments. Yet, this overlooks how segregation reshapes the provision of public goods of places that implicate the well-being of all residents. This paper links 1905–1920 birth cohorts in the 1940 full-count Census to Social Security death records (N = 2.4 million) and uses multiple instruments and a sibling fixed effects identification strategies to estimate the effect of segregation on the longevity of Black and of White Americans. Results reveal a 10-point increase in segregation reduces \textit{both} Black and White longevity—by 0.62 and 0.53 years, respectively. I show that tax revenue and welfare and health sepending explain substantial portions of these effects for Blacks and modest amounts for Whites. These results emphasize the political and policy consequences of segregation that extend beyond neighborhoods and challenge arguments that segregation produces White advantage.

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
\doublespacing

Racial residential segregation is one of the most durable features of American cities and a reminder of enduring racial stratification. A long tradition of scholarship documents how segregation concentrates disadvantage in Black neighborhoods while simultaneously sheilding White ones, and that these unequal exposures serve as mechanisms linking segregation to gaps in income, education, and health \citep{massey_american_1993,massey_effect_1987,williams_racial_2001}. Along this line of reasoning, it is often hypothesized that segregation persists because Whites necessarily benefit from it, such that Black disadvantage is the source of White advantage \citep{massey_getting_1995}. Thus, inequalities in outcomes are thought to be the result of a zero-sum process. Zero-sum beliefs about the structure race relations are salient Whites' public opinions opposing redistribution, conservative voting patterns and instrumental in their resistance to residential and school integration \citep{wetts_privilege_2018,gilens_race_1996,crowder_spatial_2008, enos_what_2016}. 

Yet, Black-White gaps themselves do not inherently equate to a zero-sum process. The White advantage hypothesis is actually one of several underlying relationships consistent with inequalities; segregation could create mutual, but unequal harm or have no effect on White outcomes and produce the same pattern. Yet, the evidence base for these the White advangage hypothesis has received less empirical attention compared to the documentation of effects on Black outcomes. While, neighborhood-based theories of segregation almost by definition lend themselves to zero-sum relationships, past research has largely overlooked the links between racial stratification, political economy \citep{dubois_black_1935}, and inequalities in spending on public goods \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016,manduca_tax_2025}. Segregation polarizes local politics and depresses the fiscal and redistributive capacity (e.g., taxes, health spending, welfare generosity) of places, the consequences of which implicate individuals beyond their immediate neighborhoods \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016,chyn_effects_2026,ananat_segregation_2009}.

In this paper, I attend to these gaps in the literature by estimating the causal effect of racial segregation on the longevity of Black and White Americans separately to identify race-specific effects and assess how much of that effect is attributable to political-economic features of segregated places. To do so, I link individuals in the 1905--1920 birth cohorts in the 1940 full-count Census to Social Security mortality records from the CenSoc Numident file (N = $2{,}412{,}213$) covering deaths between 1988 and 2005 \citep{ruggles_ipums_2023,breen_censoc_2023}. I leverage three complementary identification strategies to triangulate the plusibly causal effects of segregation; I utilize an instrumental variable strategy using the configuration of the pre-1911 railroad network within counties \citep{ananat_wrong_2011}, a second instrumental variable strategy using the density and number of natural rivers and streams \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016}, and a sibling fixed effects design that identifies the effect from differences in the residential contexts of siblings. The two instruments rest on different histories of spatial division, and the sibling design provides a test of the sensitivity of estimates to exclusion restriction violations. 

Results reveal that segregation shortens the lives of Black \textit{and} White Americans. In preferred specifications, a ten-point increase in dissimilarity reduces Black longevity by $0.62$ years and White longevity by $0.53$ years. These results remain consistent across complementary identification strategies and are robust to a battery of robustness checks that probe exclusion restriction violations, subgroup heterogeneity, mortality selection, and alternative measures of racial segregation. At the same time, I show that segregation reduces county tax revenue, health spending, and welfare spending, and that these fiscal channels accounts for $33.5$ percent of the Black effect and $12.7$ percent of the White effect. Taxes absorb larges portions of these effects alone, but welfare only helps explain effects for Blacks; these estimates imply that segregation widens the Black-White longevity gap by roughly $0.09$ years, but does so through unequally distributed harm. By shifting from studying Black-White gaps to the group-specific levels that generate gaps, these findings contribute to literature on how racial segregation affects life chances \citep{massey_american_1990,massey_effect_1987,hendi_estimation_2023} and challenge accounts of segregation and racial stratification premised on material gains for Whites \citep{massey_getting_1995,blalock_1967}. 

 Additionally, I also contribute to demographic research on the causal inputs of longevity that has largely been studied correlationally or limited to single states \citep{logan_segregation_2018,karbeah_racial_2023}. These findings extend historical accounts of political economy and racial stratification \citep{dubois_black_1935} to the setting of contemporary racial segregation \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016,chyn_effects_2026} and link these relationships to a robust literature on the mortalitly consequences of politics and policy \citep{montez_us_2026,goodman-bacon_public_2018,torche_political_2021}.

\section{Segregation and Black-White Inequalities}
% =====================================================================

Residential segregation by race is widely understood as a visible manifestation of Black-White inequalities of various forms \citep{blokland_outside_2008} and a mechanism of persistent racial stratification \citep{massey_effect_1987}. Black-White inequalities in the U.S. ranging from income to education to mortality are stark in size and troubling because their durability over time. Accordingly, research has focused extensively on the role of segregation in perpetuating Black-White inequalities in health and economic wellbeing.

A long scholarly tradition on racial segregation has detailed the adverse outcomes of racial segregation for Blacks  \citep{hendi_where_2024,massey_effect_1987,frey_central_1979}. A leading hypothesis is that segregation is both deleterous for Blacks and explicitly advantageous for Whites. The clearest evidence for this assertion can be seen in seminal work on raical segregation and violence: "racial segregation persists in the United States because whites benefit from it. In undermining the status and well-being of African-Americans and deepening their social problems, segregation simultaneously increases the incentives for whites to maintain the residential status quo" \cite[p.~1229]{massey_getting_1995}. Despite this, empirical evidence for this claim remains understudied \citep{brown_structural_2024,quillian_does_2014}.

Scholarly and public understanding of segregation suggests a particular relationship with Black-White inequalities; that of White gains from segregation at the expense of Black losses. Though hypotheses and conceptualizations vary across studies, the expectation is that segregation should be, on average, advantageous to Whites \citep{light_segregation_2019,quillian_does_2014,massey_getting_1995}. Despite an appearance of equivalency between inequalities and zero-sum relationships, Black-White inequalities are consistent with at least three underlying processes visualized in Figure \ref{fig:zero-sum-processes}. These processes are that (1) segregation is harmful to both groups, (2) segregation advantages Whites and disadvantages Blacks (zero-sum), (3) segregation disadvantages Blacks but does not affect Whites, yet existing papers rarely distinguish these explanations or explore the their consequences for understanding the mechanisms of inequality \citep{wrigley-field_three_2025} \footnote{It is important to note that zero-sum relationships are used in varied ways in the literature on racial stratification and segregation as short hand for White advangates and Black disadvantages \citep{mcghee_sum_2021}. This is worth noting because the original game theoretic definition states that zero-sum relationships are ones where gains of one individual are exactly proportional to the losses of the other \citep{von-neumann_theory_1944}. In this paper, I use zero-sum as a shorthand for relationships that create White advantage and simultaneously Black disadvantage.}.

Part of the reason that zero-sum expectations are widely accepted is that segregation scholarship focuses on the consequences of living in racially segregated neighborhoods within segregated places. A voluminous literature shows that Black residents are more likely to live in high poverty neighborhoods, be zoned for lower quality schools, experience higher crime exposure, and navigate more hazardous environmental exposures \citep{wodtke_toxic_2022,elliott_historical_2013,massey_effect_1987,wilson_truly_1987,massey_american_1993,wodtke_are_2023,goldsmith_schools_2009,light_segregation_2019} compared to White residents. Neighborhood-based theories of segregation almost by definition lend themselves to zero-sum relationships because Black and White residents are sorted into different neighborhoods with risks disproportionatley concentrated in Black neighborhoods. Yet, whether this premise holds empirically is rarely justified.

The research that has directly examined the effects of segregation on White outcomes has yielded inconsistent results across different outcomes that appear related to their degree of linkage to neighborhood-level processes. However, prior studies have attempted to test specific mechanisms, neighborhood or otherwise, in an effort to understand what may explain these findings. For instance, \citep{light_segregation_2019} find that segregation reduces White homicide victimization and increases Black homicide victimization, therefore consistent with a zero-sum relationship. While \cite{quillian_does_2014} finds that segregation is negatively associated with Black educational attainment and not associated with White educational attainment. On the other hand, \citep{cutler_are_1997} find that segregation is negatively associated with Black educational, economic, and employment outcomes, but range from slightly negatively to slightly positively associated with Whites' outcomes. Each of these relationships result in Black-White inequalities in outcomes, despite documenting different race-specific effects. Evidence for a zero-sum relationship is clearest with homicide victimization, which is sensible because crime is strongly spatially patterned and proximity is necessary for risk. In contrast, education and economic outcomes are slightly less spatially-patterned, but are varied in their strength of evidence supporting zero-sum relationships. 

The implications of how segregation affects Whites are consequential. Zero-sum expectations about racial stratification shape Whites' public opinions on race \citep{wetts_privilege_2018,bobo_attitudes_1996} and their opposition to redistributive policy \citep{gilens_race_1996,mcghee_sum_2021}; recent work shows that Black Americans hold such views as well \citep{warren_zero-sum_2025}. These expectations are central to Whites' historical resistance to residential and educational integration, premised on the belief that desegregation would harm them \citep{crowder_spatial_2008,frey_central_1979,rich_backlash_2025,johnson_children_2019}. Whether that premise is true is an empirical question. If Whites do benefit, desegregation would close Black-White gaps partly at White expense. If segregation instead harms Whites, this calls into question competition-based accounts of racial threat, which presume material rivalry \citep{blalock_1967}. Group-position accounts correctly locate opposition in relative standing rather than material stakes \citep{blumer_race_1958,bobo_attitudes_1996}; but they explain why Whites defend segregated arrangements without interrogating whether those arrangements impose material costs. Moreover, neighborhood sorting alone cannot make both groups worse off; harm to Whites and Blacks alike instead points to processes operating at a scale beyond neighborhoods.

\section{Racial Segregation and Political Economy}

Politics and policy have been central instruments of racial stratification throughout American history. Reconstruction governments sought to create universal public goods funded through general taxation, such as free public schools \citep[p.~638]{dubois_black_1935}. The counter-revolution that followed dismantled Black political power in substantial part by attacking this fiscal system: taxation was reduced, expenditure curtailed \citep[chap.~14]{dubois_black_1935}, and literacy tests and poll taxes disenfranchised Black voters and many poor Whites alongside them \citep[p.~6]{perman_struggle_2001}. Modern causal evidence confirms this account: counties where Black citizens held political power levied higher per capita taxes during Reconstruction and experienced the sharpest declines in taxation and bureaucratic capacity after Redemption, as elites built cross-class White coalitions against taxation itself \citep{logan_black_2020,chacon_democratization_2020,suryanarayan_slavery_2021}.

Despite the fact that fiscal contraction eroded public institutions that served White residents as well as Black ones \citep{margo_race_1990}, why did White workers sustain the racial order that produced it? W.E.B. Du Bois's argues that working-class Whites were symbolically compensated. Despite material conditions similar to those of Black workers, White workers received a ``public and psychological wage'' of deference and status in place of, not in addition to, the universal public goods a equitable labor relations a cross-racial coalition of workers would have secured \citep[pp.~700--701]{dubois_black_1935}. Racial division thus served as a mechanism that made fiscal retrenchment politically sustainable.

Contemporary racial segregation bears many similarities to this political-economic process, with spatial separation serving as an "linchpin" of racial stratification \citep{massey_residential_2018}. Segregation reduces contact between racial groups and heightens the salience of racial threat \citep{blumer_race_1958,blalock_1967}, increasing racial resentment and conservative voting among non-Black voters \citep{ananat_segregation_2009}. Accordingly, \cite{chyn_effects_2026} finds that segregation reduces support for the minimum wage, redistributive spending, welfare, health spending, and police reform. Segregation increases the political polarization of places, which undermines collective political efforts and in turn, spending on public services \cite{trounstine_segregation_2016}. As in the post-Reconstruction South, the resulting policy regimes harm lower-income residents of all races and with segregation serving as a substitute for material provision \citep{metzl_dying_2019}. 

To examine these consequences of segregation and facilitate a concrete discussion of mechanisms, I focus on racial disparities in mortality. Racial disparities in mortality are striking in both contemporary and historical perspective and a salient differentiationin the life chances of racial groups. In 2023, the difference in life expectancy at birth between White and Black Americans was of 4.4 years \citep{arias_united_2020}. Disparities in Black and White lifespans have remained especially persistent in light of exceptional increases in life expectancy in the last 50 years. In addition to their function as health indicators, lifespan represents an important cumulative measure of life chances \citep{weber_economy_1922} and an important axis of social inequality \citep{wrigley-field_stolen_2024}. Longevity disparities carry implications social networks, collective memory, culture, political participation, and social movements that would otherwise manifest if not for shortened lives of Blacks \citep{wrigley-field_three_2025}.

Scholarship in sociology and demogarphy has argued that racial segregation is a fundamental cause of health \citep{williams_racial_2001} with extensive documentation of the negative health effects of segregation for Black Americans and wide Black-White health disparities. Evidence on the race-specific effects of segregation on health reveal ambigous effects for Whites. \cite{austin_does_2016} found that segregation reduced the birthweight of both Black and White infants, while \cite{vu_born_2024} found negative effects on the birthweight of Black infants and no effect for White infants. Evidence on mortality is similarly inconsistent; \cite{logan_segregation_2018} found that, in certain contexts, segregation was associated with greater mortality risk for White Americans and lower risk for Black Americans, whereas \cite{karbeah_racial_2023} found that segregation increased mortality risk among both Black and White children. These divergent conclusions partly reflect methodological limitations, including reliance on death records from a single state \citep{logan_segregation_2018}. More fundamentally, existing evidence is largely correlational. Segregation covaries with features of places that are consequential for population health, thus correlational analyses cannot distinguish whether the observed relationships between segregation and mortality are causal.

\subsection{Neighborhood Effects}

According to the fundamental cause perspective, racial segregation is thought to operate through many downstream mechanisms, ranging from individual to contextual exposures. These mechanisms are often interlocking and compound over the life course \citep{breen_longevity_2024,diprete_cumulative_2006}. An exhaustive review of these machanisms is beyond the scope of this study; I summarize three predominant neighborhood-level mechanisms that describe the major differentiators of Black and White neighborhoods and their implications for health \footnote{In this section, I consider both general pathways that link segregation to health as well as those particular to older adults. This is because the Data elaborated later in this study provides a snapshot of segregation observed when an individual is 65 years old.}.

A leading pathway linking segregation to health is through concentrated poverty. Segregation increases poverty concentration \citep{massey_american_1993} and concentrates it in primarily Black neighborhoods while White neighborhoods are sheilded from it \citep{quillian_segregation_2012, ananat_wrong_2011}. Poverty affects health directly through its reduction of economic resources and employment opportunities. Poverty also matters for health indirectly, because poverty increases individual and environental stressors associated with worse health. For older adults, it is particularly consequential for the health and aging through reductions in access to health care as well as psychosocial stress. Stress is shown to accelerate aging processes \citep{massey_neighborhood_2018} and compound existing racial inequalities in health at older ages, particularly after 60 years old \citep{geronimus_weathering_2006}.

The unequal concentration of crime and policing are also central mechanisms of segregation because of their strong spatial patterning and uneuqal distribution in predominantly Black neighborhoods while insulating predominantly White ones \citep{bell_anti-segregation_2020,light_segregation_2019}. Beacuse these exposures are racialized, segregation simultaneously \textit{increases} the risk of homicide victimization for Black residents and \textit{decreases} it for White residents \citep{light_segregation_2019}. This spatial concentration is consequential for health because exposure to community violence increases risk of death \citep{edwards_risk_2019}, elevates depressive symptoms, and increases psychological distress \citep{foell_exposure_2021}. Older adults are especially vulnerable; racialized exposure to crime operates beyond acute risk of victimization to create a culture of fear \citep{klinenberg_heat_2003} that shapes physiological and psychological health. The chronic stress of residing in high-violence environments also contributes to accelerated physiological weathering \citep{geronimus_weathering_1992}. 

Finally, racial segregation affects the quality of housing and the resources households can extract from them to invest in health \citep{breen_longevity_2024}. Segregation creates competition in housing markets that impose distinct costs on Black and White residents alike, though through different mechanisms. For Whites, \cite{daniels_influence_1975} documents that White renters intent on living in predominantly White neighborhoods paid a premium to segregate themselves from Black neighbors, thus the maintenance of segregaiton cost Whites economically. For Black homeowners, buyers historically paid high premiums to purchase homes on majority-White neighborhoods and subsequently saw those homes lose value, eroding wealth prospects \cite{akbar_racial_2025}. Because housing is a key mechanism of wealth accumulation, cost burdens translate into diminished resources that affect health status \citep{hurd_health_2003,wang_housing_2026}. Segregation also degrades the quality housing, particulalry for Black residents, which has important implications for disease risk, particularly for older adults \citep{evans_residential_2024}. Lower-quality housing that is poorly insulated or lacking in suitable amenities are associated with poor health, disability at older ages, and elevated biomarkers like c-reactive protein that proxy for stress \citep{wang_housing_2026,clair_housing_2019}. 

\subsection{Political Economy of Places}
Increasingly, the political polarization of places and their libearalism or conservatism of policies matter for health and wellbeing \citep{montez_us_2026,torche_political_2021}. At the county-level, important services that shape population health are delivered, such as spending on insfrastructure, health, welfare, and the level of tax revenue generated that carry similar implications \citep{manduca_tax_2025,debenedictis-kessner_politics_2020}. Empirical research showing segregation decreases spending on public goods has implications for health and wellbeing \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016,chyn_effects_2026}. Taxation, spending on health and public services \citep{alesina_public_1999}, and the generosity of the safety net are shaped by voters within segregated places \citep{debenedictis-kessner_politics_2020, tausanovitch_representation_2014}. The county-level consequences carry the scope to shape the health of all residents of a jurisdiction regardless of the neighborhood they occupy.

There are a variety of county-level expenditures that matter for health, but the most direct are health and medicaid spending. Greater spending on public insurance programs have been shown to have profound effects on motality; for instance, \citep{goodman-bacon_public_2018} found that early medicaid adoption reduced the mortality of infants substantially and non-White child mortality by $20$ percent. Moreover, medicaid expansion was found to have particularly salient effects for older adult mortality, with \citep{miller_medicaid_2021} estimating that expansions tied to the Affordable Care Act reduced mortality by $9.4$ percent.

The level of tax revenue generated by places is critical mechanism through which places can finance policies and programs that redistribute wealth and mitigate poverty and inequality. Tax revenue operates as a general mechanism that can exacerbate or mitigate inequality across places that in turn affect health \citep{rauscher_variation_2022,obrien_fiscal_2025}. \cite{mccullough_government_2016} find that higher tax revenue among other spending categories, was associated with improvements in population health and \citep{mays_evidence_2011} found that increased local public health spending reduced preventable deaths by $6.9$ percent. More generally, \citep{lobao_poverty_2012} found that greater government fiscal capacity was associated with reductions in poverty and increases in job growth which directly shape health outcomes.

Beyond the general tax base, welfare spending encompasses the range of transfers and services that counties provide to their poorest residents that vary considerably across counties \citep{bruch_consequences_2018}. For instance, \citep{fishback_births_2007} found that New Deal relief spending during the Great Depression reduced infant mortality, suicides, and deaths from infectious disease in a panel of $114$ cities. Cash transfers have been shown to have direct implications for longevity; for instance, \citep{aizer_long-run_2016} found that the sons of mothers accepted to the Mothers' Pension program, the first cash welfare program in the U.S.,lived approximately one year longer than the sons of rejected applicants.

Despite a burgeoning evidence base demonstrating the importance of the public spending mechanisms of segregation, scholarship in sociology has rarely tested the extent to which these mechanisms explain the effects of segregation on the outcomes, despute compelling linkages. Determining how the effects of segregation operate and through whcih micro and macro-level channels is of central importance \citep{sharkey_where_2014}. This is especially relevant in the context of recent findings tha document the rising spatial scale of segregation that reflects the separation of racial groups between places \citep{carlson_changing_2026,lichter_toward_2015}. While macro-segregation currently characterizes about half of the metropolitan areas in the U.S. in 2020 \citep{carlson_changing_2026}, the increasing overlap between racial composition and macro-level geographies demands a greater udnerstanding of how political dimensions of segregation affect individual outcomes. 

\section{The Present Study}

The present study encompasses two empirical goals. First, I estimate the causal effect of segregation on the longevity of Black and White Americans using a set of instrumental variable strategies that induce greater geographic sorting by virtue of quasi-random spatial boundaries and complenetary sibling fixed effects designs leveraging within-family variation in neighborhood environments. In models stratified by race, I examine how racial segregation affects Black and White longevity separately to learn about the specific relationships that underlie Black-White inequalites. This approach to analyzing mortality disparities provides a quantification of \textit{how} segregation affects Black and White longevity. The zero-sum expectation requires that the sign of the estimate on segregation is negative for Blacks and positive for Whites, would the relationship between segregation and longevity be considered zero-sum. From these analyses, I am able to recover Black-White differences in longevity from the estimates and differentiate between the underlying processes that generate inequalities.

Second, I test to the extent to which several measures of county-level public goods  explain the observed relationships between segregation and longevity. These mechanisms are (1) county tax revenue, (2) medicaid spending and health expenditures, and (3) welfare and cash assistance spending. While far from comprehensive, these different measures of county policy support provide a direct asessement of the level of policy liberalism with constructs and measures analagous to those used at the state-level \cite{montez_us_2020} and have storng theoretical links to individual health outcomes. These mechanisms provide a set of pathways through which segregation could affect individuals regardless of residence and therefore help explain part of the effects of segregation on mortality for Blacks and Whites alike. This effort builds upon prior scholarship documenting how segregation erodes support for progressive policies and reduces public spending \citep{chyn_effects_2026,trounstine_segregation_2016} by testing their explanatory power for the relationship between segregation and mortality.

\section{Data}
% =====================================================================

This study links six sources of data together to study the relationship between racial segregation and mortality: (1) individual-level data from the 1940 Census, which contains demographic information (race, gender, education, birth year); (2) linked mortality records from the CenSoc Numident data file, which contain information on year of death, place of death, and place of birth \citep{breen_censoc_2023}; (3) county-level summaries of the racial composition and segregation of places from the decennial census (1980-2000) \citep{ruggles_ipums_2023}; (4) data on historical railroad line shapefiles established before 1911 \citep{atack_historical_2016}, which are used to construct the first set of instruments; (5) county-level data on the arrangement of rivers, streams, and waterways from the TIGER/Line linear hydrography files \citep{census_tiger_2023}, which are used to construct the second set of instruments; and (6) government finance data from the Government Finance Database \citep{pierson_government_2015}, which are used to calculate county-level expenditures and tax revenues to test political-economic mechanisms.

The data cover deaths occurring between 1988 and 2005 among members of the 1905--1920 birth cohorts.
I link these sources such that each observation represents an individual and includes their demographic characteristics measured in 1940, county of birth, year and county of residence at death, and the segregation, contextual, instrument, and policy measures of the county in which they died.

\subsection{Linked 1940 Census and Numident Mortality Data}

The empirical analyses in this paper are anchored by individual-level demographic data records from the 1940 Full Count Census \citep{ruggles_ipums_2023}. To construct my analytic sample, I identify individuals from the 1905-1920 birth cohorts who were 20-35 in 1940.
These individuals are then linked to Social Security Administration (SSA) mortality records between 1988 and 2005 provided by the Berkeley CenSoc Numident file \citep{breen_censoc_2023}.
These data offer unparalleled individual-level data on mortality that are geolocated to contemporary residence at death and which are representative of the population living in the United States in 1940. The geographic specificity and rich covariates make these data ideal for analyzing inequalities in mortality.
CenSoc Numident records cover deaths occurring between 1988 and 2005, allowing for reliable estimates of longevity conditional on an individual surviving to age 65 \citep{goldstein_mortality_2023}. The interpretation of longevity conditional on surviving to 65 arises because of the death window that is reliably covered by the Numident sample \citep[p.~35]{breen_censoc_2023} and is the interpretation used in \citet{breen_longevity_2024}.. The implications of this condition for the results are addressed directly in Section 8.4.

I focus on the 1905-1920 birth cohorts for two reasons. First, their birth years and the end year of the mortality observation window allow for plausibly complete observation of mortality that is not biased by right censorship (i.e. missing the oldest old).
In other words, those born in 1905 would be 100 years old in 2005, making it reasonable to assume that few individuals remain alive. Even for those individuals who live very long, using doubly truncated data will result in estimates biased toward zero \citep{breen_longevity_2024}, and thus produce estimates of the effect of segregation on longevity that are likely conservative.

Second, restricting to ages 20-35\footnote{\citet{halpern-manners_effects_2020} finds that the mean number of years of education of decedents in the 1940 Census is 10.4 years (1525). If we assume a normative time of starting school at 6, individuals in the sample reach the average at 16.4 years, which is well before our age cutoff.} allows for confidence that individual-level confounders to longevity, such as education status, are observed.
By conditioning on those 20 years or older, it is reasonable to assume that individuals have completed the modal level of formal education by the time that they appear in the sample.

Despite the strengths of the Numident data, there are limitations worth noting. The first is double truncation, where data are unobserved on both the left and right side of the death window (e.g., before 1988 and after 2005).
\citet{goldstein_mortality_2023} show that when not accounted for, doubly truncated mortality observation windows result in biased estimates of longevity disparities that shrink toward 0 and higher mean observed death age in samples. \footnote{The high average age at death for both Black and White individuals can be seen in Table 1 transparently and this is an artifact of truncation.}
This means that those born in 1905 are observed between the ages of 83-100 years old and those born in 1920 are observed between the ages of 68-85 years old.
Birth year fixed effects help to account for some of this bias \citep{goldstein_mortality_2023}, where individuals of varying birth cohorts may have differing numbers of years covered by the death window. However, this cannot be fully accounted for in analyses. Fortunately, a quantification of the bias is available using a Gompertz maximum likelihood technique \citep{goldstein_mortality_2023,breen_longevity_2024}, which I implement and return to in Section 8.4. 

The second limitation concerns race-specific representativeness. The CenSoc Numident file links 1940 Census data to the public release of the National Archives Record Administration ``NARA Numident'' file using the Abramitzky, Boustan, and Eriksson (ABE) exact matching algorithm \citep{abramitzky_europes_2012}. For the Numident sample, 22\% of the cases in the 1940 Census are matched to mortality records, which is concordant with other historical census matching efforts \citep[p.~7]{breen_censoc_2023}. A natural question is whether the differences in match rates are sufficient to produce bias in results when studying mortality with the Numident linked data. For Black men and women, comparisons with the 1940 Census indicate that the Numident file slightly underrepresents these groups, at $-1.8$ and $-2.4$ percent differences, respectively \citep{breen_censoc_2023}. \footnote{This matching procedure may be particularly selective for unmarried women, because of marriage-related name changes over the life course. Fortunately the Numident data contain parents' last names that can be used to link women who have undergone name change.} \citep{breen_censoc_2023} argue that bias due to representivity difference is modest when analyses are stratified by race. Nevertheless, \citet{osborne_censoc-numident_nodate} provide a set of post-stratification weights that re-weight CenSoc Numident counts to period-specific distributions of deaths to adjust for lingering selection from matching differences. To address this, I re-run models using these weights are included in Appendix Table A2.  

Additionally, analyses using sibling information, draw on a subsample of the analytic sample in which siblings can be identified within 1940 households. Siblings are identified using the ABE algorithm according to the procedure in \citep{breen_longevity_2024}, but produces a much smaller sample size than the full Numident data. The differences in individual characteristics of these samples are discussed in later sections. 

\subsection{Segregation Exposure and County Characteristics at Death}

I examine exposure to segregation in mid-to-late life, obtained and calculated based on the county individuals resided in at death. Examining the county at death offers an empirical understanding of the effects of segregation that occurred after the Great Migration and the passage of Civil Rights Act that outlawed legal segregation.
Because contemporary segregation scholarship examines the consequences of de jure segregation, this time period aligns more consistently with these theories. Scholars recognize that the geographic scope and structure of segregation after the Great Migration are understood to be distinct from the segregation that occurred before, predominately in the South \citep{leibbrand_great_2020,grigoryeva_historical_2015}.

There are of course limitations to using county at death. It is reasonable to believe that other exposures that occur throughout the life course, such as the place characteristics of where individuals grew, up affect their health and mortality long after. To address these potential exposures, I include birth county fixed effects to absorb early life characteristics. Yet, it is nevertheless likely that segregation in the county at death net of birth county fixed effects still miss intervening exposures between birth and death matter for health. Patterns of neighborhood attainment and residential mobility over the life course show that the modal neighborhood transitions are to places with similar characteristics \citep{sampson_neighborhood_2008}. Moreover, across the life-course, migration propensity declines with age \citep{bernard_life-course_2014}: individuals are much less likely to migrate after age 40 and, when they do, it is usually short distances \citep{molloy_internal_2011,zimran_internal_2022}. For these reasons, I argue that the county of death plausibly represents a period of exposure encompassing mid-to-late life, but this is not verifiable due to data constraints. 

To measure segregation, I use the dissimilarity index (D) \citep{duncan_methodological_1955}.
The dissimilarity index measures the evenness of the distribution of Black and White residents within a county, ranging from 0-100, and is interpreted as the share of Black or White individuals that would have to move to a different neighborhood for the racial composition of neighborhoods to be the same as the racial composition of the county.
The dissimilarity index is widely used in prior work \citep{hendi_where_2024,light_segregation_2019,massey_american_2003,cutler_are_1997} because it is straightforward to calculate, concordant with past empirical evidence, and has a concrete interpretation.The dissimilarity index for the county an individual resided in at the time of their death is calculated formally in Equation (1):

\begin{align} 
D = \frac 1 2 \sum_i^N | \frac{b_i}{B} - \frac{w_i}{W}| \times 100 
\end{align}

Where N is the number of counties, $b_i$ and $w_i$ are the proportion of Black and White residents in the $i$th census tract and B and W are the proportion of Black and White residents in the county.
The dissimilarity scores are calculated from decennial Censuses 1980, 1990 and 2000, which correspond to the year of death observed for sample members. \footnote{Although widely used and the interpretation convenient the D is well known to suffer from various index biases, outlined in \citet{fossett_new_2017} and others \citep{winship_revaluation_1977,carrington_measuring_1997}, particularly as they relate to temporal comparisons and variability in the population composition and relative group size of counties. Accordingly, in supplemental analyses, I replicate the main results with a bias-adjusted D index that adjusts for this bias \citep{fossett_new_2017}. In contrast to D, the bias-adjusted D measure is more complex to estimate and the interpretation less concrete \citep[p.~233]{fossett_new_2017}. For these reasons, I use the conventional dissimilarity index for main analyses.}. 

\subsection{Railroad and Waterway Instrument Data}

I construct the instruments used in analyeses from two sources of geospatial data.
First, I use Jeremy Atack's historical GIS database of U.S. railroads, which digitizes the network of rail lines placed in operation between 1826 and 1911 \citep{atack_historical_2016}.
Retaining all segments in operation by 1911, I overlay the historical rail network on county boundaries and construct the Railroad Division Index (RDI) developed by \citet{ananat_wrong_2011}, which is described in detail in the Methods section. I also compute an indicator for any pre-1911 railroad, total track length, and track density (kilometers of track per square kilometer of land area).

Second, I measure the arrangement of rivers and streams within counties using the U.S. Census Bureau's TIGER/Line linear hydrography shapefiles \citep{census_tiger_2023}, in the spirit of the topographical instruments introduced by \citet{cutler_are_1997}. From these files, I retain natural stream and river features and exclude man-made canals, ditches, and artificial connectors, which are plausibly endogenous to development.

\subsection{Public Spending Data}

To measure county-level political-economic mechanisms, counties are linked to government finance records from the Government Finance Database (GFD), a compilation of Census of Governments and Annual Survey of Governments finance data \citep{pierson_government_2015}.
From the GFD, I extract the finances of county governments and construct measures of total tax revenue (including property and individual income tax components), health and hospital expenditures, Medicaid vendor payments, education spending, and direct welfare and cash assistance spending. Because government units are surveyed at varying frequencies, finance records are summed within each county-year and then averaged within the decade windows corresponding to the death decades observed in the sample (1980--1989, 1990--1999, and 2000--2005), so that each individual is assigned the policy environment of the decade in which they died. Each measure is expressed per 1{,}000 county residents using decennial census population denominators. Dollar values are inflation adjusted to 2000 dollars. Appendix table A1 provides descriptive statistics for the county-level policy measures used in the analyses.

The resulting counties are filtered from all counties in the U.S. such that they have a Black population greater than 0 and coverage of the instruments \footnote{The data filtering procedure and stepwise sample sizes are included in Table 3.}. \section{Empirical Strategy}
% =====================================================================

This section outlines the main analysis plan to estimate the causal effect of racial segregation on the longevity of Black and White Americans. Existing studies provide compelling evidence of associations between segregation and poor health outcomes, but these studies are largely correlational. There are many features of places such as infrastructure, environmental factors that jointly determine segergation patterns and population health. At the individual level, as highlighted by early work \citep{massey_effect_1987}, segregation is theorized to be both a mechanism of racial stratification as well as an outcome of racial stratificiation that shapes the educational and economic status of individuals. Causal estimates of segregation are necessary, because correlational estimates cannot isolate the direct effects of segregation on health from confounding processes at both the macro and micro levels. 

To estimate the causal effects of segregation on longevity, I use a set of instrumental variables (IV) and a sibling fixed effects designs. The logic of these instruments is to leverage quasi-random natural and historical variation in physical barriers that divide up counties to instrument for racial segregation. Greater spatial division of a county, thus facilitates more segregation within places \footnote{Similar instrumental variable designs have been used to examine the effect of segregation on educational outcomes \citep{cutler_are_1997,quillian_does_2014}, poverty and life expectancy \citep{ananat_wrong_2011,hendi_where_2024}, political composition \citep{ananat_segregation_2009}, and public spending \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016}.}. 
Instrumental variable designs are powerful because they offer one strategy to identify the effects of segregation in a setting where segregation patterns are otherwise impossible to experimentally manipulate. They also offer a high degree of geographic representivity in the sample but with assumptions that can be hard to test. My approach in this paper is to traingulate the plausibly causal effect of segregation on longevity using a vriety of complementary strategies anchored by the IV estimates. I first describe the necessary IV asusmptions and evidence in support of their validiity, then I present a complementary instrument strategy and an alternative sibling fixed effects strategy.

IV designs rely on three core assumptions. First, relevance requires that the instruments strongly predict segregation \citep{angrist_one_2021}. Second, the exclusion restriction requires that instruments affect longevity only through segregation. \footnote{Because there are more instruments than endogenous regressors, a natural test of the validity of many instruments are overidentification tests. These tests are commonly interpreted as testing the ``validity'' of the instruments, however overidentification tests must first assume that one instrument is valid (e.g., in the just-identified case) \citep{felton_handle_2024}, which is not informative for assessing validity. A more informative interpretation from \citet{mogstad_causal_2021} is that rejection of overidentification tests suggests that there is heterogeneity in treatment effects across subpopulations affected by the different instruments, which is not in reference to validity. Additionally, overidentification tests assume constant treatment effects, which is unreasonable in a setting where the IV estimate is a local average treatment effect. As expected, the instruments generally fail to pass these tests given the large sample size of my data, where observations are in the millions. An alternative to overidentification tests is placebo analyses that require a researcher find an additional instrument that shares all the same confounders as the original instrument but is thought to not affect the treatment \citep{felton_handle_2024}. While informative, the shared confounders assumption of placebo instruments is also quite strong and the search for a candidate placebo instrument faces the same challenges as the search for a credible instrument.} Third, monotonicity requires that the instruments push segregation in the same direction for all units. While a direct test of this assumption is not possible, I examine demographic patterns that would be consistent with monotonicity and discuss them in the appendix Figure \ref{fig:iv_monotonicity}. Conditional on these assumptions, IV estimates are interpreted as the local average treatment effect (LATE) \citep{imbens_identification_1994,mogstad_causal_2021}, or the treatment effect among those living in counties where segregation increased because of greater physical division.

The preferred instrumental variable strategy is the railroad division index (RDI) proposed by \citep{ananat_wrong_2011}. This instrument is constructed for counties \footnote{This has been used prior in \citep{quillian_does_2011,ananat_segregation_2009,chyn_effects_2026} at the metro-level.} using historical railroad shapefiles sourced from \citep{atack_new_1994}. The RDI measures the extent to which counties are divided up by intersections historical railroad lines that were established in the early 20th Century. The RDI calculates the number of intersections that a county has with rail lines in a standardized index that ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating more division. In addition to rail lines, I also instrument with the density of railroad tracks per square kilometer of the county. The calculation of the RDI is described as follows: 

\begin{align} 
RDI = 1 - \sum_i (a_i/A)^2
\end{align}
 Where $a_i$ is the area of each subpolygon created by cutting the county with the rail network and $A$ is the total county area. The index equals 0 for counties left undivided by railroads and approaches 1 as rail lines subdivide a county into many small pieces, capturing the physical partitioning of places that facilitated racially divided neighborhood boundaries.

The intuition behind the RDI is that historical railroads provided natural barriers within places that populations could cluster around, thus creating higher levels of baseline segregation. \citep{ananat_wrong_2011} also demonstrates that historical White segregationist efforts often utilized natural barriers such as rail lines to establish communities and neighborhoods, because these barriers are naturally difficult to navigate. 

There are two leading issues that may undermine exclusion. First, in its original usage \citep{ananat_wrong_2011}, the RDI is constructed for Northern metropolitan areas, because the majority of African Americans lived almost entirely in the South before the 1900s when the railroads were laid. Therefore only in non-Southern cities can railroad configuration be treated as exogenous to race and thought to induce segregation by Black inflows from the great migration. This the logic of exclusion rests on this restriction but limits the geographic generalizability of estimates. To overcome this, I adjust for residence in the South in all models and test the robustness of these results using an alternative set of instruments described in this section as well as sibling fixed effects estimates that do not require exclusion restrictions. 

Second, while railroad division does not directly relate to railroad \textit{exposure} per se, it could be indirectly related. These instruments use \textit{configuration} of barriers within a county rather than exposure to the barriers themselves. Despite the exposure-division distinction, configuration could violate exclusion. The exclusion restriction hinges on the assumption that early 20th Century railroads do not affect mortality directly. Railroads could affect mortality by generating financial prosperity in areas surrounding tracks which could promote better health. \citep{black_impact_2015}, using railroad proximity to instrument for migration, they show that railroads had little association with economic outcomes for a non-selective group of Southern-born White older adults, indicating that the long-term health impacts of railroad induced prosperity are likely minimal. To test this directly, I regress education and income of 1905-1920 birth cohorts in 1940 on the RDI to test whether railroad division is directly related to socioeconomic outcomes. I estimate these models for those that did not migrate during their lifetime to capture the potential impact of the RDI for those exposed continuously \footnote{It is possible that there are exposures that I cannot observe between birth place and death place that occured between censuses. This serves as only a proxy of continous exposure. What these estimates do gaurantee is that individual was exposed to the RDI in their place of birth temporally before attending school. While exclusion only needs to hold for the county-at-death exposure, in both sets of definitions, there is not a relationship with educational attainment.} to the same place as well as the full sample to capture the relationship between exposure to the RDI in one's county at death (Appendix Tables A6 and A7). These tests indicate that the RDI is not significantly associated with the educational attainment of Blacks or Whites, regardless of sample. However, the RDI is associated actually slightly negatively associated income for Blacks and Whites at a similar magnitude ($-.04$). However it is important to note the magnitude of this association is small. The RDI ranges from 0-1, indicating that a one point increase in railroad division (RDI/100) would translate to approximately a $0.004\%$ decrease in earnings in 1940. While these estimates are significantly negative, they are quite insignificant substantively. \footnote{It is important to highlight that the regression results for income and education for those who did not migrate during their lifetime (i.e., those that were can confidently attribute their RDI value to birth place as well as death place) are highly similar to the full sample results, suggesting that whether we capture RDI exposure early or later in life, any impacts do not appear to compound over time in ways that exacerbate exclusion restriction violations.}

On the other hand, railroads could also result in increased hazards such as pollution that harm health \citep{dockery_association_1993}. In particular, small particulate matter in the air has resulting from exhaust and emissions is associated with poor health outcomes \citep{sangkham_update_2024}. To test the extent to which the RDI could directly affect health through pollution, I examine the relationship between the RDI and small Particulate Matter (PM2.5) concentration $PM2.5/m^3$ and railroad division in U.S. counties in 1999 and 2000. \footnote{Prior to the 1990s, the Environmental Protection Agency did not collect data on PM2.5 levels. Modeled estimates are available for earlier periods, but may be subject to bias.}. PM2.5 measures the concentration of tiny dust and soot particles in the air in micrograms per cubic meter ($\mu /m^3$), with an safe range concentration ranging between 0-9 within an area. Tests of the association between RDI and PM2.5 concentration are positively and significantly associated, but the magnitude is very small. Going from a county that has no railroad division to one that is completely divided by rail lines represents a difference in PM2.5 concentration of $.356$ that, while significantly associated, is quite small in magnitude, which suggests that the direct effects on mortality are potentially minimal (Appendix Table A8).

Despite evidence presented against exclusion violations owing to direct effects of the RDI on health and the expansion of the to Southern states, it is nevertheless possible that there is still bias present. The second set of instruments employed to complement this strategy utilize the density of natural rivers and streams within a county \footnote{Each of the instruments relying on spatial division will be correlated to some degree, as demonstrated in Appendix Table A9, however the overall $R^2$ between instruments is relatively weak. The pairwise $R^2$ between the RDI and rivers is $0.002$. These relatively low values indicate that each of these instruments is picking up on a different dimension of spatial division in an area rather than substituting for another measure. }. For each county, I compute the number of distinct named rivers and  along with total stream length and stream density \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016} \footnote{Man made rivers and streams are omitted.}.

The logic of using natural streams and rivers to instrument for segregation relies on the quasi-randomness of these natural boundaries. Like the RDI, rivers and streams divide up areas in ways that shape population settlement patterns around them but are plausibly unrelated to contemporary outcomes. While railroads and highways have been used historically to segregate, those boundaries are man-made, which raises the concern that they could be engogenous to segregation. Natural boundaries are not subject to these sources of bias because they are difficult for humans to manipulate. Rivers and streams also overcome the issues posed by generalizing the RDI to the South, because they do not rely on any particular history of settlement. It is still possible, however, that more rivers and streams are correlated with greater availability of green spaces and natural areas that carry health benefits, thus violating the exclusion restriction. 

Thus far, different instrument strategies carry the shared concerns that geographic arrangements could affec health. To complement these IV approaches, I utilize a sibling fixed effects identification strategy that does not rely on any of these exclusion restrictions \footnote{Sibling fixed effects strategies have been used in prior literature to estimate the effects of education on mortality \citep{halpern-manners_effects_2020} and the effects of neighborhoods on intergenerational mobility \citep{chetty_impacts_2018}}. Sibling designs identify effects by controlling for unobserved heterogeneity at the family-level by differencing out shared factors common among siblings (e.g., family wealth, health endowments, genetic factors, etc.). After controlling for individual demographic characteristics that vary across siblings, these designs identify the effect of segregation on longevity from differences in siblings residential environments.

The sibling fixed effects strategy is advantageous in this study context, because it relies on different assumptions and sources of identifying variation to establish a causal relationship. While sibling fixed effects require more modest assumptions, they also carry important considerations regarding external validity, due to their reliance on a subset of matched sibling pairs and discordance in residential environments among siblings. Fortunately, observable characteristics of the sibling sample track the full sample closely (as shown in Table 1), providing confidence in their representivity compared to the broader population.

Each of these designs rely on different identifying variation and assumptions for identifying causal effects. Because sibling designs do not require the same exclusion restriction assumptions as the instruments, then agreement across designs suggest that exclusion violations do not drive results, which allows for the triangulation of the effect of segregation on longevity.

\subsection{Estimation Strategy}

The estimation strategy in this paper revolves around three sets of models startified by race: OLS regression models, Instrumantal Variable regression models, and sibling fixed effects models. First, I estimate a set of OLS regression models of the form: 

\begin{align} 
&\text{(OLS)} \quad Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta Segregation_{i} +  \pmb{\beta} X_i + \gamma_{b(i)} + \lambda_{c(i)} + \epsilon_i 
\end{align}

Where $\beta segregation$ is the dissimilarity in an individual’s county at death. In all models, segregation is expressed on a scale from $0-100$ so that the estimate corresponds to each unit increase in dissimilarity rather than the contrast of a perfectly integrated or segregated city. $\pmb{\beta}$ is a vector of regression coefficients and $X$ is a vector of individual-level control variables and $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ are fixed effects for individual birth year and birth county

Second, I estimate a set of Instrumental Variable regressions via two-stage least squares estimation according to the following form: 

\begin{align}
&\text{(First-Stage)} \quad \beta Segregation_{i} = instrument \in
\begin{cases}
\small RDI + Rail\ Length/km^2, \\
N\ Waterways + Waterway\ Length/km^2
\end{cases} & \\
&\text{(IV)} \qquad\quad\ \ Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta Segregation_{i} + \pmb{\beta} X_i + \gamma_{b(i)} + \lambda_{c(i)} + \epsilon_i & \nonumber
\end{align}

Where the form of the model is the same as OLS, however, now segregation has been instrumented by one of the instrument sets. In contrast to regression models estimated by OLS, the coefficient on segregation changes to the local average treatment effect of segregation on the longevity among compliers: those whose counties became more segregated in response to a denser configuration of physical barriers.

Third, I estimate the sibling fixed effects models on the subsample of sibling data using the same regression specification as equation (3), however, in this model an identifier that groups individuals into siblings within families is added. The models are estimated as follows: 

\begin{align} 
&\text{Sibling FE} \ Y_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta Segregation_{i} +  \pmb{\beta} X_i + \gamma_{b(i)} + \lambda_{c(i)} \sigma_{d(j)} + \epsilon_i 
\end{align}

Where $\sigma_{d(j)}$ is a family fixed effect and the indexing of the model has been adapted to index individuals $i$ within families $j$. 

In all models, I control for a rich set of individual and contextual covariates to adjust for any residual confounding.
In fully specified models, I control for sex, education, marital status, employment status, whether an individual migrated between birth and death, whether they reside in the South, urban-rural fixed effects \footnote{Between 1993 and 2003, Rural-Urban Continuum codes changed from their definitions between 1983-1993. For 1983-93 codes, metro counties were classified using a scheme ranging from 0-3, with counties that belonged to a metro area with a population of 1 million or more were categorized into Central and Fringe counties. In 2003, this scheme was revised so that counties were classified based only by population, with Fringe and Central collapsed into one category. Accordingly, to increase comparability. I collapse codes 0 and 1 in the 1983 and 1993 continuum code schemes into one category so that they are harmonized 1-3 scheme used in 2003.}, birth year and state fixed effects, and occupation fixed effects. 

It is important to note the multi-level structure of these data: segregation is measured at the county level, while 
individuals are clustered within counties. Because the regressor of interest is 
constant within cluster and errors are plausibly correlated within cluster, default 
standard errors are potentially severely downward-biased \citep{moulton_illustration_1990, 
cameron_practitioners_2015}. In all models I report cluster-robust standard 
errors at the county level. This addresses the same source of dependence that a 
random-intercept multilevel model would absorb, but does so without requiring that the within-cluster error 
covariance structure be correctly specified, which is the condition under which the feasible generalized least squares and multilevel estimator's efficiency and inferential validity hold \citep{cameron_practitioners_2015}. Since my interest is in the county-level association rather than in variance decomposition, the clustering solution is preferred because it imposes fewer assumptions on the error process.

\section{Results}
% =====================================================================
I present the main results in the order described in the Empirical Strategy. Tables of regression coefficients used to produce all figures are available in the Appendix. I begin with the relevance of the instruments, because the credibility of the instruments rest on this assumption. I then report the OLS associations between segregation and longevity separately for Black and White individuals, which establish the sign and magnitude of the relationship in the dataset. I then turn to the instrumental variable estimates and sibling fixed effects estimates to triangulate the effects of segregation on longevity across empirical strategies. Throughout, I estimate all models separately by race and express the estimates as the effect of a 10-point increase in the dissimilarity index.

First, the relevance assumption requires that the instruments strongly predict segregation. Figure \ref{fig:first-stage} displays binned scatterplots of the first-stage relationship between each instrument and county dissimilarity, residualized on the instrument in each strategy \footnote{The binscatter plot is a method to intuitively visualize bivariate relationships in large datasets. Because there are $2,955$ unique counties, examining a scatterplot with individual points can be hard to visualize. The binscatter approach bins the distribution of the independent variable and then take the average of the outcome variable in each bin. These binned $y-x$ relationships are then visualized in a scatterplot to approximate the relationship. Full regression results are available in the Appendix.}. Panel A shows that counties more granularly divided by the pre-1911 railroad network are substantially more segregated: a county at the top of the RDI distribution is roughly $7$--$8$ points more segregated than an undivided county. \subsection{Main Estimates of Segregation on Longevity}

Next, I present main estimates of the OLS association between segregation and longevity across samples by race and the IV and sibling fixed effects estimates. Figure \ref{fig:ols-all} reports OLS association between segregation and longevity, estimated separately for Black and White decedents with birth year, birth state, and urban-rural fixed effects, and with the addition of individual controls and occupation fixed effects. Because the samples used by each identification strategy differ, the details of which are described in the Appendix, I re-estimate the OLS association on each of the three estimation samples that the subsequent models use. In the IV sample, the association is consistently negative for both groups regardless of the sample considered and with the inclusion of controls. Among Black individuals, a 10-point increase in dissimilarity is associated with $0.73$ fewer years of life; among White individuals, the same increase is associated with $0.65$ fewer years.\footnote{These are the OLS fits estimated on the IV estimation sample with the same covariates and fixed effects as the IV models, so that the only thing that changes between Figure \ref{fig:ols-all} and the instrumental variable estimates is the estimator itself. The corresponding column of the OLS regression table is estimated on the full analytic sample without the Southern residence indicator and gives a slightly smaller association for Black decedents ($-0.71$).} A key takeaway from Figure \ref{fig:ols-all} is that the associations are remarkably insensitive to which sample it is estimated on. Across the IV, flexible sibling, and exact sibling samples, the Black estimate ranges from $-0.73$ to $-0.61$ years and the White estimate remains stable across models at $-0.65$ years per 10-point increase in dissimilarity. The Black estimates are less precise in the sibling samples, which is expected given that the sibling subsamples contain between $3{,}344$ and $4{,}652$ Black individuals, depending on the matching procedure, compared with $125{,}074$ in the IV sample. Whatever variation in sample composition the identification strategies introduce, it does not by itself move the association. 

Recall that Figure \ref{fig:zero-sum-processes} described three stylized race-specific relationships between segregation and longevity that are consistent with Black-White disparities. These OLS associations provide initial support in favor of interpretation (A), mutual harm from segregation, rather than a zero-sum relationship, which would require a positive coefficient for Whites. The relevant feature of these estimates is the sign pattern: both coefficients are negative in every sample, which is the pattern Panel (A) predicts and is inconsistent with the White advantage that Panel (B) requires. These associations are nevertheless descriptive. Individuals sort into counties for reasons plausibly related to their health and county-level features may jointly determine both segregation and population health, thus I turn to the instrumental variable and sibling fixed effects estimates to recover the causal effect.

The IV designs recover a local average treatment effect among compliers and depend on an exclusion restriction that cannot be tested directly; the sibling fixed effects design requires no exclusion restriction but identifies the effect only from siblings who reside in counties with different levels of segregation and in a much smaller sample. If the estimates converge despite these differences, it is unlikely that any single design's assumptions are generating the result. Across specifications, the results are quite consistent. The estimates are negative and significant in every specification, with some variability in magnitude. The instrumental variable estimates are generally more conservative than the sibling fixed effects estimates, the exception being the rivers specification, which is also the least precisely estimated of the four strategies. The consistent takeaway across models is that higher segregation means shorter lives for Black and White Americans alike, which challenges the White advantage hypothesis.

Because all models are estimated separately by race, the Black and White coefficients cannot be compared statistically within any single specification.\footnote{Race-stratified estimation means that the Black and White coefficients come from different models fit to different samples, so no test of their difference is available. What can be assessed is whether the same qualitative ordering recurs across designs that rest on different identifying assumptions.} What can be assessed is whether estimates agree across designs. In three of the four strategies: the RDI instrument and both sibling fixed effects specifications reveal a larger Black penalty above the White one. The rivers instrument is the lone exception, and it is the strategy whose confidence intervals are by far the widest. Evaluated holistically, the evidence therefore points toward a larger longevity penalty for Black Americans.

Under fully-adjusted RDI estimates, a 10-point increase in dissimilarity widens the Black-White longevity gap by $0.09$ years. The gap and the individual-level regressions tell different stories about the ostensibly same relationship. This longevity gap arises not from a zero-sum process, but from lowering the longevity of both groups. A naive analysis would recover a Black disadvantage of about a month, while missing the conext that both groups are disadvantaged by segregation. 

How should one understand the magnitude of these effects substantively? In the context of contemporary demographic research on the social determinants of longevity, these effects are substantial. Consider the effects of homeownership and years of education. \citep{halpern-manners_effects_2020} reports that each additional year of education increases longevity by $.34$ years using sibling fixed effects models and the 1940 full count Census. In comparison to RDI estimates, the effect of increasing segregation by 10 points is of similar magnitude to the effects of attaining approximately two additional years of education. \citep{breen_longevity_2024} additionally estimates that being a homeowner versus a renter in early adulthood increases longevity by $.31$ years. These estimates of the effect of segregation are thus equivalent to twice the magnitude of homeownership. Though these estimates are quantitatively large, it is important to situate this 10-point contrast within the change to segregation over time. In sample counties in 1980 the mean dissimilarity was $46.14$ and by 2000 it was $35.90$, a $10.24$ point decline. In other words, the 10-point change represents what the effect of moving from the average segregated county in 1980 to the average segregated county in 2000.

\subsection{Political-Economic Mechanisms of Segregation}

What explains the effects of segregation on longevity for White and Black Americans? In this paper, I argue that political-economic mechanisms play an important role in shaping the outcomes of individuals because of their macro-level pathways tied directly to health and wellbeing. To test the importance of several of these mechanisms, I conduct a stepwise analysis of mechanisms compared to the baseline adjusted RDI IV regressions (equation 4) \footnote{A descriptive table on the segregation mechanisms tested here are displayed in Appendix Table A1.}. I start with estimates using the RDI stratified by race and include each set of mechanisms which are then compared to the baseline adjusted model. 

The logic of the exercise is the following. If segregation shortens lives partly by reshaping the fiscal and redistributive capacity of the places people live in, then conditioning on those policy channels should absorb part of the reduced-form effect recovered in the previous section. If instead the effect operates entirely through channels unrelated to local political economy, adding these controls should leave the coefficient on $D$ unchanged. I consider four county-level channels measured over the 1980, 1990, and 2000 decennial panels: total taxes per capita, direct welfare expenditure per capita, health and hospital expenditure per capita, and Medicaid vendor payments per capita, all constructed from county government finance records. Each model adds one channel at a time to the fully-adjusted RDI specification with a final specification fully adjusting for all policy variables, and all models are re-estimated on the subsample of counties with non-missing policy data ($102{,}605$ Black and $2{,}098{,}307$ White decedents) so that the baseline and the mechanism models are directly comparable.

\input{../FigTab/mechanism_ols_iv_table.tex}

Table 2 displays OLS and IV regression estimates of the aggregate relationship between racial segregation and public spending outcomes. In the IV specification, racial segregation is negatively associated with tax revenue, health spending, and welfare spending, but does not appear to be related to Medicaid spending. Across OLS and IV models, both specifications produce estimates with the same sign with the exception of the estimate for welfare, which flips its sign. However,it is important to note that the estimates of the relationship between segregation and welfare are noisy.

Figure \ref{fig:mechanisms} reports estimates of the effect of segregation on longevity by race after adding each of the mechanisms to individual-level regression models. Each estimate is the coefficient on $D$ from the fully-adjusted RDI IV model, and the label to the right of each interval expresses it as a percentage of that race's baseline estimate, in which no mechanism is controlled. Values below 100 percent indicate that the channel absorbs part of the effect of segregation on longevity; values at or above 100 percent indicate that it absorbs none of it.

Because these models are restricted to counties with non-missing policy data, their baseline estimates are slightly smaller than the headline RDI estimates reported above: a 10-point increase in dissimilarity reduces longevity by $0.58$ years for Black decedents and $0.48$ years for White decedents on this subsample. All percentages below are expressed relative to these subsample baselines rather than to the full-sample estimates.

First, fiscal channels absorb a non-trivial share of the effect for both Blacks and Whites. Controlling for county taxes per capita reduces the coefficient on segregation to $78.6$ percent of its baseline value among Black decedents and to $84.4$ percent among White decedents. The share absorbed by the tax base is also the mechanism that absorbs as similar share of the total effects for both groups, consistent with theory about their broad based effects. In aggregate, segregation sharply reduces tax revenue by roughly \$812 per thousand residents in the IV specification.

Second, direct welfare expenditure is sharply asymmetric by race. It absorbs about a fifth of the Black estimate ($82.4$ percent) and none of the White estimate ($101.0$ percent). The racial asymmetry in these relationships is not surprising considering the large proportions of the Black population receiving welfare relative to Whites. The
consequence of this asymmetry is th that most of the racial difference in total mediated share ($33.5$ versus $12.7$ percent, a difference of $20.8$ points) is attributable to how welfare absorbs the effects of segregation differentially by race. Thus, because segregation reduces welfare spending, this differentialy harms Black residents above and beyond the general fiscal contraction that harms both groups.

Third, health and hospital expenditures barely shift estimates for Black or White individuals; for Whites the coefficient is marginally larger than baseline once health spending is included ($104.9$ percent). Medicaid vendor payments absorb slightly less than a tenth of the effect for either group ($92.6$ and $95.5$ percent).

When considering policy portfolios of places, these joint mechanisms account for $33.5$ percent of the estimate of segregation on longevity for Blacks and $12.7$ percent of the estimate for Whites. Tax revenue is the single largest channel for both groups, absorbing $21.4$ percent of the Black estimate and $15.6$ percent of the White estimate on its own. For Black decedents the remaining spending channels add to what taxes absorb, whereas for White decedents they do not; the joint specification absorbs slightly less than taxes alone. What clearly stands out for both groups is that the general fiscal capacity of places, rather than any single expenditure category, plays the substantial role in attenuating the effects of segregation on health.

These estimates should be read as bounds on mediated shares rather than as point estimates of one. The policy channels are measured post-treatment, so conditioning on them can introduce bias from intermediate confounders that jointly determine local policy and longevity, and the confidence intervals on the mechanism-adjusted coefficients overlap with the baseline coeffcient \footnote{It is important to remember, however, that the estimates can only be compared qualitatively rather than statistically because they are estimated using separate models.}. Nevertheless, this exercise supports the claim that the political economy of segregated places is a substantial explanatory pathway relating segregation to outcomes. These political economy pathways account for about $13$ percent of the longevity penalty for White Americans and  $33$ percent of the penalty for Black Americans. These estimates are consistent with the argument that segregation affects places in ways that extend beyond invidual neighborhood contexts.

\subsection{Double Truncation and Mortality Crossover}

Thus far the estimates presented suggest that segregation had significant, negative effects on the longevity of both Black and White Americans aged 65 and older. However, because surviving to 65 years and older is selective, this raises the possibility that race-specfic processes of mortality selection could bias these estimates. A large literature in demography focused on the descriptively-named "Black-White mortality crossover" has found that advanced ages, positively-selected Black older adults have lower mortality risk compared to White older adults of the same age. In this setting, it could be perfectly feasible that the magnitude of the negative estimates for Whites could be accounted for by this selection and the magnitude of the estimates for Blacks could be a significant underestimate of the full magnitude. I present two peices of evidence against this conclusion. 

Literature on the Black-White mortality cross-over generally finds that the crossover occurs at advanced ages. Prior scholarship has generally found that the crossover occurs between the mid-to-late 80s (see \citep{breen_black_2026} for a review) while the majority of this sample is between the ages of 67-82, bias from the cross-over only plausibly affects the oldest members in the sample. To provide a quantification of the bias induced by truncated data, \citep{goldstein_mortality_2023} developed a Gompertz maximum likelihood estimation procedure to model partially observed cohorts while explicitly accounting for truncation. The Gompertz procedure takes into account the truncated nature of the data and re-estimates the associations recovered by a traditional regression model using a log of the Gompertz distribution to correct estimates. This allows for a more accurate estimation of longevity and characterization of group-differences in longevity, which allows a quantification of the potential for the mortality cross-over to bias estimates directly. 

Figure \ref{fig:gompertz} provides a graphical representation of my main regression estimates by race in comparison to the Gompertz-corrected estimates. The Gompertz estimates are obtained by modeling death age as a function of segregation, birth year, birth state, and urban-rural fixed effects. This estimate is then paired with an identical OLS regression of the same form for a direct comparison of the difference between estimates. The bias induced by double truncation is minimal in magnitude and results reach the same conclusions as the main estimates. The Gompertz-corrected estimate for Black longevity is $8$ percent larger than the regression-based estimate and the White longevity estimate is $6$ percent larger. This increase in the magnitude is consistent with prior scholarship that estimates with truncated data are biased toward zero. These corrected estimates are thus slightly larger, but recover the same substantive Black-White longevity gap estimated via race-stratified regression models.

\section{Discussion}
% =====================================================================

Racial segregation is an important mechanism of racial stratification that affects the health and wellbeing of Black populations. Conversely, popular and scholarly discourse hypothesizes racial segregation to generate Black-White inequality by advantaging Whites while simultaneously disadvantaging Blacks. Despite the ubiquity of this explanation, Black-White disparities are actually consistent with several distinct processes other than the White advantage hypothesis. Namely, segregation could harm Whites as well or could have no effect on their outcomes. While most of the literature on segregation emphasizes the role of neighborhoods as mechanisms that could generate zero-sum relationships between segregation and outcomes, a burgeoning interdisciplinary literature has documented how racial segregation shapes the political economy of places in profound ways by lowering spending on public goods that have implications for residents regardless of their neighborhoods. In this paper, I first test the argument that racial segregation shapes Black-White disparities by creating White advantage by examining the effect of racial segregation on the longevity of Black and White Americans using complete count 1940 Census data and several complementary identification strategies. I then use county-level government finance data to explore how much political-economic mechanisms contribute to segregation effects.

\subsection{Monotonicity}

 It is well known that segregation levels have declined over time. If part of that decline reflects less economic and racial residential sorting, then we would expect the association between physical division and segregation to weaken year-over-year. Following \citet{rauscher_why_2020}, evidence for monotoniciy would suggest that the instrument strength declines over decades. Figure \ref{fig:iv_monotonicity} displays $R^2$ coefficients from first-stage regressions for the RDI and rivers instruments by death decade. Consistent with this assumption, the strength of the association between the instruments and segregation, measured by $R^2$, decreases between 1980 and 2000. While this does not represent a conclusive test, declining $R^2$ coefficients over time for the rivers instrument are consistent with the monotonicity assumption. The coefficients for the RDI decrese between 1980-1990 and then increase slightly back to the 1980 baseline by 2000, suggesting that the relationship between the instruments and segregation is relatively stable over time.

\subsection{Heterogeneity by Cohort, Gender, and Education}

Thus far, the main estimates suggest overall that segregation harms Black and White individuals' longevity in substantial ways. How do these patterns vary by important social axes? In this section I consider variation by birth cohorts, military service (for men), education levels, and gender to probe the consistency of these results. 

\subsection{Birth Cohort and Military Service}

It is possible that military service and coming of age during a period when the U.S. was at war could have profound selective effects on individuals that could create heterogeneity in these estimates. In this section, I present estimates on birth cohort-stratified models and for a subset of men who served in the military. Examining birth cohorts serves two purposes. The first is to probe how the results vary when considering individuals who were born in earlier versus later historical periods; the second is to provide a partial asessment of the selective impacts of growing up during wartime periods on longevity for the population overall. While no cohorts in my sample are completely untouched by periods of significant wartime combat, examining effects by birth cohort provides a partial asessment of the experiences of individuals who experienced more or less war. That is, those born earlier in the sample likely experieneced both World War I and World War II, while those born more recently only experienced World War I. Furthermore, I also merged information from the CenSoc Numident Army Enlistment records \citep{breen_censoc_2023} on to these data to examine the heterogeneity of the main estimates for men in the sample who served and did not serve. Appendix figures \ref{fig:iv_bc-all} and \ref{fig:iv_mil-all} present birth cohort stratified estimates and estimates interacted by military service respectively.
