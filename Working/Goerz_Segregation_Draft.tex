\section{Abstract}
% =====================================================================

Racial segregation is widely understood to advantage White Americans at the expense of Black Americans through stratified neighborhoods. However, this overlooks how segregation reshapes the provision of public goods and fiscal capacity of entire places that affect residents beyond neighborhoods. This paper links 1905--1920 birth cohorts in the 1940 full-count Census to Social Security death records (N = 2.4 million) and uses multiple instruments and a sibling fixed effects identification strategy to estimate the effect of segregation on the longevity of Black and White Americans. Results reveal that a 10-point increase in segregation reduces \textit{both} Black and White longevity by 0.57 and 0.44 years, respectively. I show that tax revenue and welfare spending act as primary mechanisms for explaining these effects for Blacks and modestly for Whites. These results emphasize the political and policy consequences of segregation that extend beyond neighborhoods and challenge expectations that Whites materially benefit from segregation.

\section{Introduction}
% =====================================================================
\doublespacing

Racial residential segregation is one of the most durable features of American cities and a mechanism of enduring racial stratification. A long tradition of scholarship documents how segregation concentrates disadvantage in Black neighborhoods while shielding White ones. These unequal exposures serve as mechanisms linking segregation to gaps in income, education, and health \citep{massey_american_1993,massey_effect_1987,williams_racial_2001}. Along this line of reasoning, it is often hypothesized that segregation persists because Whites necessarily benefit from it, such that Black disadvantage is simultaneously a source of White advantage \citep{massey_getting_1995}. Therefore, racial inequalities in outcomes are thought to reflect a zero-sum relationship between segregation and outcomes.

The White advantage hypothesis that underlies a zero-sum process is actually one of several underlying relationships consistent with inequalities; segregation could create mutual, but unequal harm or have no effect on White outcomes and produce the same inequalities. Yet, the evidence base for the White advantage hypothesis has received less empirical attention compared to the documentation of effects on Black outcomes. While neighborhood-based theories of segregation almost by definition lend themselves to zero-sum relationships, past research has largely overlooked the links between racial segregation, political economy, and inequalities in spending on public goods \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016,manduca_tax_2025}. Segregation polarizes local politics and depresses the fiscal and redistributive capacity (e.g., taxes, health spending, welfare generosity) of places, the consequences of which implicate individuals beyond their immediate neighborhoods \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016,chyn_effects_2026,ananat_segregation_2009}.

In this paper, I attend to these gaps in the literature by estimating the causal effect of racial segregation on the longevity of Black and White Americans and assess how much of that effect is attributable to political-economic features of segregated places. To do so, I link individuals in the 1905--1920 birth cohorts in the 1940 full-count Census to Social Security mortality records from the CenSoc Numident file (N = $2{,}377{,}343$) covering deaths between 1988 and 2005 \citep{ruggles_ipums_2023,breen_censoc_2023}. I leverage three complementary identification strategies to estimate the plausibly-causal effect of segregation: instrumental variable strategy using the configuration of the pre-1911 railroad network \citep{ananat_wrong_2011}, a second instrument using the density of natural rivers and streams \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016}, and a sibling fixed effects design that identifies the effect from differences in the residential contexts of siblings.

Results reveal that segregation shortens the lives of Black \textit{and} White Americans. In preferred specifications, a 10-point increase in dissimilarity reduces Black longevity by $0.57$ years and White longevity by $0.44$ years. These results remain consistent across complementary identification strategies and are robust to a battery of robustness checks that probe exclusion restriction violations, subgroup heterogeneity, mortality selection, and alternative measures of racial segregation. At the same time, I show that segregation is linked to reductions in county tax revenue, health spending, and welfare spending. These fiscal pathways mediate an appreciable portion of the effect for both groups, but their explanatory power is not evenly distributed by race, accounting for a substantially larger share of the Black effect. Taxes alone absorb large portions of these effects, but welfare helps explain effects only for Blacks; these estimates imply that segregation widens the Black-White longevity gap by roughly $0.13$ years, but does so through unequally distributed harms. By studying race-specific effects of segregation that generate gaps, these findings contribute to literature on how racial segregation affects life chances \citep{massey_american_1990,massey_effect_1987,hendi_estimation_2023} and challenge accounts of segregation and racial stratification premised on material gains for Whites \citep{massey_getting_1995,blalock_1967}. Methodologically, I also contribute to demographic research on the causal inputs of longevity, where research regarding segregation has largely been studied correlationally or limited to single states \citep{logan_segregation_2018,karbeah_racial_2023}.

\section{Segregation and Black-White Inequalities}
% =====================================================================

Racial residential segregation is widely understood as a visible manifestation of Black-White inequalities of various forms \citep{blokland_outside_2008} and a mechanism of persistent racial stratification \citep{massey_effect_1987}. Black-White inequalities in the U.S. ranging from income to education to mortality are stark in size and durable over time. Accordingly, research has focused extensively on the role of segregation in perpetuating Black-White inequalities in health and economic wellbeing.

A long scholarly tradition on racial segregation has detailed the adverse outcomes of racial segregation for Blacks \citep{hendi_where_2024,massey_effect_1987,frey_central_1979}. A widely accepted hypothesis is that segregation is both deleterious for Blacks and explicitly advantageous for Whites. The clearest evidence for this assertion can be seen in seminal work by Douglas Massey on racial segregation and violence: ``racial segregation persists in the United States because whites benefit from it. In undermining the status and well-being of African-Americans and deepening their social problems, segregation simultaneously increases the incentives for whites to maintain the residential status quo'' \citep[p.~1229]{massey_getting_1995}. Despite this assertion, empirical evidence for this claim remains understudied \citep{brown_structural_2024,quillian_does_2014}.

Scholarly and public understanding of segregation suggests a particular relationship with Black-White inequalities; with the expectation that segregation should be, on average, advantageous to Whites \citep{light_segregation_2019,quillian_does_2014,massey_getting_1995} and disadvantageous to Blacks. Despite inequalities and zero-sum relationships appearing interchangeable, Black-White inequalities are consistent with at least three underlying processes visualized in Figure \ref{fig:zero-sum-processes}. These processes are that (1) segregation is harmful to both groups, (2) segregation advantages Whites and disadvantages Blacks (zero-sum), and (3) segregation disadvantages Blacks but does not affect Whites. Yet existing papers rarely distinguish these explanations or explore their consequences for understanding the mechanisms of inequality \citep{wrigley-field_three_2025}.\footnote{It is important to note that zero-sum relationships are used in varied ways in the literature on racial stratification and segregation as shorthand for White advantages and Black disadvantages \citep{mcghee_sum_2021}. This is worth noting because the original game theoretic definition states that zero-sum relationships are ones where gains of one individual are exactly proportional to the losses of the other \citep{von-neumann_theory_1944}. In this paper, I use zero-sum as a shorthand for relationships that create White advantage and simultaneously Black disadvantage.}

Part of the reason that zero-sum expectations are widely accepted is that segregation scholarship focuses on the consequences of living in racially segregated neighborhoods within segregated places. A voluminous literature shows that Black residents are more likely to live in high poverty neighborhoods, be zoned for lower quality schools, experience higher crime exposure, and navigate more hazardous environmental exposures \citep{wodtke_toxic_2022,elliott_historical_2013,massey_effect_1987,wilson_truly_1987,massey_american_1993,wodtke_are_2023,goldsmith_schools_2009,light_segregation_2019} compared to White residents. Neighborhood-based theories of segregation almost by definition lend themselves to zero-sum relationships because Black and White residents are sorted into different neighborhoods with risks disproportionately concentrated in Black neighborhoods.

Research examining the effects of segregation on White outcomes has yielded inconsistent conclusions, and this variation appears to be related to how tightly each outcome is linked to neighborhood context. For instance, \citet{light_segregation_2019} find that segregation reduces White homicide victimization while increasing Black victimization, consistent with a zero-sum relationship. By contrast, \citet{quillian_does_2014} finds that segregation is negatively associated with Black educational attainment but unrelated to White attainment, and \citet{cutler_are_1997} find that segregation harms Black economic and educational outcomes while its effects on Whites' outcomes are variable. Because each of these studies employs a plausible identification strategy, methodological differences are unlikely to explain this variation alone. A more likely explanation is the spatial structure of the outcomes themselves. When the exposure that affects an outcome is tightly-linked to neighborhood context, such as homicide victimization, physical proximity to violence is necessary for risk. Therefore the sorting of racial groups into separate neighborhoods mechanically shifts that risk from White to Black residents. When an outcome instead depends on processes that operate beyond the neighborhood, such as school systems, labor markets, and place-level policy, spatial separation does not buffer Whites from segregation to the same degree, which makes the effects on outcomes theoretically ambiguous. This linkage is plausible but untestable with existing evidence, because studies rarely test the mechanisms through which segregation operates. Thus, it remains unknown whether variation across studies reflects different mechanisms, different outcomes, or both. This ambiguity regarding variation raises questions about the extent to which zero-sum frameworks accurately describe the consequences of segregation.

The implications of how segregation affects Whites are consequential. Zero-sum expectations about racial stratification shape Whites' public opinions on race \citep{wetts_privilege_2018,bobo_attitudes_1996} and their opposition to redistributive policy \citep{gilens_race_1996,mcghee_sum_2021}; recent work shows that Black Americans hold such views as well \citep{warren_zero-sum_2025}. These expectations are central to Whites' historical resistance to residential and educational integration, premised on the belief that desegregation would harm them \citep{crowder_spatial_2008,frey_central_1979,rich_backlash_2025,johnson_children_2019}. Whether that premise is true is an empirical question. If Whites do benefit, desegregation would close Black-White gaps partly at White expense. If segregation instead harms Whites, this calls into question competition-based accounts of racial threat, which presume rivalry over material conditions \citep{blalock_1967}. Group-position theory on the other hand centers the importance of relative standing rather than material stakes \citep{blumer_race_1958,bobo_attitudes_1996}, but these accounts do not explain why Whites defend segregated arrangements when they may impose material costs. Moreover, neighborhood sorting alone cannot make both groups worse off; harm to Whites and Blacks alike instead points to processes operating at a scale beyond neighborhoods.

\section{Racial Segregation and Political Economy}

Politics and policy have been central instruments of racial stratification throughout American history. Reconstruction governments sought to create universal public goods funded through general taxation, such as free public schools \citep[p.~638]{dubois_black_1935}. The counter-revolution that followed dismantled Black political power in substantial part by attacking this fiscal system: taxation was reduced, expenditures curtailed \citep[chap.~14]{dubois_black_1935}, and literacy tests and poll taxes disenfranchised Black voters and many poor Whites alongside them \citep[p.~6]{perman_struggle_2001}. This is corroborated by empirical studies showing that counties where Black citizens held political power levied higher per capita taxes during Reconstruction and experienced the sharpest declines in taxation and bureaucratic capacity after Redemption, as elites built cross-class White coalitions against taxation itself \citep{logan_black_2020,chacon_democratization_2020,suryanarayan_slavery_2021}.

Despite the fact that fiscal contraction eroded public institutions that served White and Black residents alike \citep{margo_race_1990}, why did White workers sustain the racial order that produced cross-racial harm? Historical accounts of Reconstruction by W.E.B. Du Bois argue that working-class Whites were symbolically compensated. Despite material conditions similar to those of Black workers, White workers received a ``public and psychological wage'' of deference and status in place of the universal public goods and equitable labor relations that a cross-racial coalition of workers would have secured \citep[pp.~700--701]{dubois_black_1935}. Racial division thus served as a mechanism facilitating elite political interests and fiscal retrenchment.

Contemporary racial segregation bears many similarities to this political-economic configuration, with spatial separation serving as a ``linchpin'' of racial stratification \citep{massey_residential_2018}. Segregation reduces contact between racial groups and heightens the salience of racial threat \citep{blumer_race_1958,blalock_1967}, increasing racial resentment and conservative voting among non-Black voters \citep{ananat_segregation_2009}. Accordingly, \citet{chyn_effects_2026} finds that segregation reduces support for the minimum wage, redistributive spending, welfare, health spending, and police reform. Segregation thus increases the political polarization of places, which undermines collective political efforts and, in turn, spending on public services \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016}.\footnote{While not about segregation, \citet{obrien_redistribution_2017} shows that racial composition also shapes the tax progressivity of places, which may bear similar features to segregation.} As in the post-Reconstruction South, the resulting policy regimes harm lower-income residents of all races, with segregation serving as a substitute for material provision \citep{metzl_dying_2019}. 

To examine these consequences of segregation and facilitate a concrete discussion of mechanisms, I focus on racial disparities in mortality. Racial disparities in mortality are striking in both contemporary and historical perspective and a salient differentiator of the life chances of racial groups. In 2020, the difference in life expectancy at age 65 between White and Black Americans was $2.4$ years \citep{arias_united_2022}. Disparities in Black and White lifespans have remained especially persistent in light of exceptional increases in life expectancy in the last 50 years. In addition to its function as a health indicator, lifespan represents an important cumulative measure of life chances \citep{weber_economy_1922} and an important axis of social inequality \citep{wrigley-field_stolen_2024}. Longevity disparities carry implications for social networks, collective memory, culture, political participation, and social movements that would otherwise manifest if not for the shortened lives of Blacks \citep{wrigley-field_three_2025}.

Scholarship in sociology and demography has argued that racial segregation is a fundamental cause of health disparities \citep{williams_racial_2001}. According to the fundamental cause perspective, racial segregation is thought to operate through many downstream mechanisms, ranging from individual risk factors to contextual exposures. These mechanisms are often interlocking and compound over the life course \citep{breen_longevity_2024,diprete_cumulative_2006}. Evidence on the race-specific effects of segregation on health reveals ambiguous effects for Whites that vary across studies. \citet{austin_does_2016} found that segregation reduced the birthweight of both Black and White infants, while \citet{vu_born_2024} found negative effects on the birthweight of Black infants and no effect for White infants. Evidence on mortality is similarly inconsistent; \citet{logan_segregation_2018} found that, in certain contexts, segregation was associated with greater mortality risk for White Americans and lower risk for Black Americans, whereas \citet{karbeah_racial_2023} found that segregation increased mortality risk among both Black and White children. 

These varied conclusions reflect methodological and data limitations that undermine conclusions about the race-specific effects of segregation. For instance, many studies rely on death records from a single state that undermine national representativeness \citep{logan_segregation_2018}. More fundamentally, existing evidence is largely correlational \citep{kramer_is_2009,white_elucidating_2012,williams_racial_2001}. Segregation covaries with features of places that are consequential for population health, and individuals sort into neighborhoods in ways shaped by their educational attainment, socioeconomic status, and structural patterns of discrimination that are themselves related to health and mortality \citep{sampson_neighborhood_2008,sharkey_where_2014}. These selection processes may not merely attenuate estimates but can reverse their sign, meaning that inconsistent findings on mortality are consistent with confounding rather than with the effects of segregation itself. Correlational analyses therefore cannot establish whether the observed relationships between segregation and mortality are causal. This paper overcomes these limitations by leveraging quasi-experimental methods to identify the causal effects of segregation on longevity, and it moves beyond single-state analyses by using nationally representative full-count Census data linked to Social Security mortality records.

An exhaustive review of these mechanisms is beyond the scope of this study; I summarize three predominant neighborhood-level mechanisms that describe the major differentiators of Black and White neighborhoods and their implications for health then shift to outlining political-economic mechanisms.\footnote{In this section, I consider both general pathways that link segregation to health as well as those particular to older adults. This is because the data described later in this study provide a snapshot of segregation observed when an individual is 65 years old.}

\subsection{Neighborhood Effects}

A leading pathway linking segregation to health is through concentrated poverty. Segregation increases poverty concentration \citep{massey_american_1993} and concentrates it in primarily Black neighborhoods while White neighborhoods are shielded from it \citep{quillian_segregation_2012, ananat_wrong_2011}. Poverty affects health directly through its reduction of economic resources and employment opportunities. Poverty also matters for health indirectly, because poverty increases individual and environmental stressors associated with worse health. For older adults, it is particularly consequential for health and aging through reductions in access to health care as well as psychosocial stress. Stress is shown to accelerate aging processes \citep{massey_neighborhood_2018} and compound existing racial inequalities in health at older ages, particularly after 60 years old \citep{geronimus_weathering_2006}.

The unequal concentration of crime and policing is also a central mechanism of segregation because of its strong spatial patterning and its concentration in predominantly Black neighborhoods, which insulates predominantly White ones \citep{bell_anti-segregation_2020,light_segregation_2019}. Because these exposures are racialized, segregation simultaneously \textit{increases} the risk of homicide victimization for Black residents and \textit{decreases} it for White residents \citep{light_segregation_2019}. This spatial concentration is consequential for health because exposure to community violence increases risk of death \citep{edwards_risk_2019}, elevates depressive symptoms, and increases psychological distress \citep{foell_exposure_2021}. Older adults are especially vulnerable; racialized exposure to crime operates beyond acute risk of victimization to create a culture of fear \citep{klinenberg_heat_2003} that shapes physiological and psychological health. The chronic stress of residing in high-violence environments also contributes to accelerated physiological weathering \citep{geronimus_weathering_1992}. 

Finally, racial segregation affects the quality of housing and the resources households can extract from them to invest in health \citep{breen_longevity_2024}. Segregation creates competition in housing markets that impose distinct costs on Black and White residents alike, though through different mechanisms. For Whites, \citet{daniels_influence_1975} documents that White renters intent on living in predominantly White neighborhoods paid a premium to segregate themselves from Black neighbors, so the maintenance of segregation carried an economic cost for Whites. For Black homeowners, buyers historically paid high premiums to purchase homes in majority-White neighborhoods and subsequently saw those homes lose value, eroding wealth prospects \citep{akbar_racial_2025}. Because housing is a key mechanism of wealth accumulation, cost burdens translate into diminished resources that affect health status \citep{hurd_health_2003,wang_housing_2026}. Segregation also degrades the quality of housing, particularly for Black residents, which has important implications for disease risk, particularly for older adults \citep{evans_residential_2024}. Lower-quality housing that is poorly insulated or lacking in suitable amenities is associated with poor health, disability at older ages, and elevated biomarkers like c-reactive protein that proxy for stress \citep{wang_housing_2026,clair_housing_2019}.

\subsection{Political Economy of Places}

The political polarization of places and the liberalism or conservatism of their policies matter for health and wellbeing \citep{montez_us_2026,torche_political_2021}. At the county level, important services that shape population health are delivered, including spending on infrastructure, health, and welfare; the level of tax revenue generated carries similar implications \citep{manduca_tax_2025,debenedictis-kessner_politics_2020}. The separation between racial groups into different neighborhoods increases political polarization along existing racial cleavages and undermines the ability for voters to find common ground on taxation and collective investments, which decreases spending on public goods \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016,chyn_effects_2026}. Taxation, spending on health and public services \citep{alesina_public_1999}, and the generosity of the safety net are therefore outgrowths of a segregated voter base \citep{debenedictis-kessner_politics_2020, tausanovitch_representation_2014}. The county-level consequences carry the scope to shape the health of all residents of a jurisdiction regardless of the neighborhood they occupy.

There are a variety of county-level expenditures that matter for health, but the most direct are health and Medicaid spending. Greater spending on public insurance programs has been shown to have profound effects on mortality; for instance, \citet{goodman-bacon_public_2018} found that early Medicaid adoption reduced the mortality of infants substantially and non-White child mortality by $20$ percent. Moreover, Medicaid expansion was found to have particularly salient effects for older adult mortality, with \citet{miller_medicaid_2021} estimating that expansions tied to the Affordable Care Act reduced mortality by $9.4$ percent.

The level of tax revenue generated by places is a critical mechanism through which places can finance policies and programs that redistribute wealth and mitigate poverty and inequality. Tax revenue operates as a general mechanism that can exacerbate or mitigate inequality across places that in turn affect health \citep{rauscher_variation_2022,obrien_fiscal_2025}. \citet{mccullough_government_2016} find that higher tax revenue, among other spending categories, was associated with improvements in population health and \citet{mays_evidence_2011} found that each 10 percent increase in local public health spending reduced preventable deaths by up to $6.9$ percent. More generally, \citet{lobao_poverty_2012} found that greater government fiscal capacity was associated with reductions in poverty and increases in job growth which directly shape health outcomes.

Beyond the general tax base, welfare spending encompasses the range of transfers and services that counties provide to their poorest residents that vary considerably across counties \citep{bruch_consequences_2018}. For instance, \citet{fishback_births_2007} found that New Deal relief spending during the Great Depression reduced infant mortality, suicides, and deaths from infectious disease in a panel of $114$ cities. Cash transfers have been shown to have direct implications for longevity; for instance, \citet{aizer_long-run_2016} found that the sons of mothers accepted to the Mothers' Pension program, the first cash welfare program in the U.S., lived approximately one year longer than the sons of rejected applicants.

Despite a burgeoning evidence base demonstrating the importance of the public spending mechanisms of segregation, scholarship in sociology has rarely tested the extent to which these mechanisms explain the effects of segregation on these outcomes, despite compelling linkages. This theoretical framework outlining public spending pathways as mechanisms of segregation yields two empirically testable implications. The first is simply support for whether public spending pathways account for any portion of the link between segregation and longevity. The second is how much of the effect they account for and how much this varies by race. For this second proposition, if segregation harms both groups equally, this provides strong support for the perspective that segregation operates through common mechanisms that supersede neighborhoods. Yet, if these mechanisms differentially account for Black and White outcomes, this suggests that place-level mechanisms also operate in racialized ways.

Determining how the effects of segregation operate and through which mechanisms is of central importance \citep{sharkey_where_2014}. This is especially relevant in the context of recent evidence documenting the rising spatial scale of segregation that reflects the separation of racial groups between places \citep{carlson_changing_2026,lichter_toward_2015}. While macro-segregation characterized about half of the metropolitan areas in the U.S. in 2020 \citep{carlson_changing_2026}, the increasing overlap between racial composition and macro-level geographies demands a greater understanding of how political dimensions of segregation affect individual outcomes.

\section{The Present Study}

The present study encompasses two empirical goals. First, I estimate the causal effect of segregation on the longevity of Black and White Americans using a set of instrumental variable strategies that leverage quasi-random spatial boundaries inducing greater geographic sorting and complementary sibling fixed effects designs leveraging within-family variation in neighborhood environments. In models stratified by race, I examine how racial segregation affects Black and White longevity separately to learn about the specific relationships that underlie Black-White inequalities. This approach to analyzing mortality disparities provides a quantification of \textit{how} segregation affects Black and White longevity. The zero-sum expectation requires that the estimate on segregation be negative for Blacks and positive for Whites. From these analyses, I am able to recover Black-White differences in longevity from the estimates and differentiate between the underlying processes that generate inequalities.

Second, I test the extent to which several measures of county-level public spending explain the observed relationships between segregation and longevity. These mechanisms are (1) county tax revenue, (2) Medicaid spending and health expenditures, and (3) welfare and cash assistance spending. While far from comprehensive, these different measures of county policy support provide a direct assessment of the level of policy liberalism with constructs and measures analogous to components used in related literature \citep{montez_us_2020} and have strong theoretical links to individual health outcomes. These mechanisms provide a set of pathways through which segregation could affect individuals regardless of residence and therefore could explain part of the effects of segregation on mortality for Blacks and Whites alike. This effort builds upon prior scholarship documenting how segregation erodes support for progressive policies and reduces public spending \citep{chyn_effects_2026,trounstine_segregation_2016} by testing their explanatory power for the relationship between segregation and mortality.

\section{Data}
% =====================================================================

This study links six sources of data together to study the relationship between racial segregation and mortality: (1) individual-level data from the 1940 Census, which contains demographic information (race, gender, education, birth year); (2) linked mortality records from the CenSoc Numident data file, which contain information on year of death, place of death, and place of birth \citep{breen_censoc_2023}; (3) county-level summaries of the racial composition and segregation of places from the decennial census (1980--2000) \citep{ruggles_ipums_2023}; (4) data on historical railroad line shapefiles established before 1911 \citep{atack_historical_2016}, which are used to construct the first set of instruments; (5) county-level data on the arrangement of rivers, streams, and waterways from the TIGER/Line linear hydrography files \citep{census_tiger_2023}, which are used to construct the second set of instruments; and (6) government finance data from the Government Finance Database \citep{pierson_government_2015}, which are used to calculate county-level expenditures and tax revenues to test political-economic mechanisms.

The data cover deaths occurring between 1988 and 2005 among members of the 1905--1920 birth cohorts.
I link these sources such that each observation represents an individual and includes their demographic characteristics measured in 1940, county of birth, year and county of residence at death, and the segregation, contextual, instrument, and policy measures of the county in which they died.

\subsection{Linked 1940 Census and Numident Mortality Data}

The empirical analyses in this paper are anchored by individual-level demographic data records from the 1940 Full Count Census \citep{ruggles_ipums_2023}. To construct my analytic sample, I identify individuals from the 1905--1920 birth cohorts who were aged 20--35 in 1940.
These individuals are then linked to Social Security Administration (SSA) mortality records between 1988 and 2005 provided by the Berkeley CenSoc Numident file \citep{breen_censoc_2023}.
These data offer unparalleled individual-level data on mortality that are geolocated to contemporary residence at death and which are representative of the population living in the United States in 1940. The geographic specificity and rich covariates make these data ideal for analyzing inequalities in mortality.
CenSoc Numident records cover deaths occurring between 1988 and 2005, allowing for reliable estimates of longevity conditional on an individual surviving to age 65 \citep{goldstein_mortality_2023}. The interpretation of longevity conditional on surviving to 65 arises because of the death window that is reliably covered by the Numident sample \citep{goldstein_mortality_2023} and is the interpretation used in \citet{breen_longevity_2024}. The implications of this condition for the results are addressed directly in Section~\ref{sec:truncation}.

I focus on the 1905--1920 birth cohorts for two reasons. First, their birth years and the end year of the mortality observation window allow for plausibly complete observation of mortality that is not biased by right censoring (i.e., missing the oldest old).
In other words, those born in 1905 would be 100 years old in 2005, making it reasonable to assume that few individuals remain alive. Even for those individuals who live very long, using doubly truncated data will result in estimates biased toward zero \citep{breen_longevity_2024}, and thus produce estimates of the effect of segregation on longevity that are likely conservative.

Second, restricting to ages 20--35\footnote{\citet{halpern-manners_effects_2020} finds that the mean number of years of education of decedents in the 1940 Census is 10.4 years (p.~1525). If we assume a normative time of starting school at 6, individuals in the sample reach the average at 16.4 years, which is well before our age cutoff.} allows for confidence that individual-level confounders to longevity, such as education status, are observed.
By conditioning on those aged 20 years or older, it is reasonable to assume that individuals have completed the modal level of formal education by the time that they appear in the sample.

Despite the strengths of the Numident data, there are a few features worth noting. The first is double truncation, where data are unobserved on both the left and right side of the death window (e.g., before 1988 and after 2005).
\citet{goldstein_mortality_2023} show that when not accounted for, doubly truncated mortality observation windows result in biased estimates of longevity disparities that shrink toward zero and higher mean observed death age in samples.\footnote{The high average age at death for both Black and White individuals can be seen in Table 1 transparently and this is an artifact of truncation.}
This means that those born in 1905 are observed between the ages of 83--100 and those born in 1920 are observed between the ages of 68--85.
Birth year fixed effects help to account for some of this bias \citep{goldstein_mortality_2023}, where individuals of varying birth cohorts may have differing numbers of years covered by the death window. However, this cannot be fully accounted for in analyses. Fortunately, a quantification of the bias is available using a Gompertz maximum likelihood technique \citep{goldstein_mortality_2023,breen_longevity_2024}, which I implement and return to in Section~\ref{sec:truncation}.

The second feature concerns race-specific representativeness. The CenSoc Numident file links 1940 Census data to the public release of the National Archives Record Administration ``NARA Numident'' file using the Abramitzky, Boustan, and Eriksson (ABE) exact matching algorithm \citep{abramitzky_europes_2012}. For the Numident sample, 22\% of the cases in the 1940 Census are matched to mortality records, which is concordant with other historical census matching efforts \citep[p.~7]{breen_censoc_2023}. A natural question is whether the differences in match rates are sufficient to produce bias in results when studying mortality with the Numident linked data. For Black men and women, comparisons with the 1940 Census indicate that the Numident file slightly underrepresents these groups, at $-1.8$ and $-2.4$ percent differences, respectively \citep{breen_censoc_2023}.\footnote{This matching procedure may be particularly selective for unmarried women, because of marriage-related name changes over the life course. Fortunately the Numident data contain parents' last names that can be used to link women who have undergone name change.} \citet{breen_censoc_2023} argue that bias due to differences in representativeness is modest when analyses are stratified by race. Nevertheless, \citet{osborne_censoc-numident_nodate} provide a set of post-stratification weights that re-weight CenSoc Numident counts to period-specific distributions of deaths to adjust for lingering selection from matching differences. To address this, I re-run models using these weights, which are included in Appendix Table A2.

Additionally, analyses using sibling information draw on a subsample of the analytic sample in which siblings can be identified within 1940 households. Siblings are identified using the ABE algorithm according to the procedure in \citet{breen_longevity_2024}, but this produces a much smaller sample size than the full Numident data. The differences in individual characteristics of these samples are discussed in later sections. 

\subsection{Segregation Exposure and County Characteristics at Death}

I examine exposure to segregation in mid-to-late life, obtained and calculated based on the county in which individuals resided at death. Examining the county at death offers an empirical understanding of the effects of segregation that occurred after the Great Migration and the passage of the Civil Rights Act that outlawed legal segregation.
Because contemporary segregation scholarship examines the consequences of \textit{de facto} segregation, this time period aligns more consistently with these theories. The geographic scope and structure of segregation after the Great Migration are understood to be distinct from the segregation that occurred before, predominately in the South \citep{leibbrand_great_2020,grigoryeva_historical_2015}.

There are of course limitations to using county at death. It is reasonable to believe that other exposures that occur throughout the life course, such as the place characteristics of where individuals grew up, affect their health and mortality long after. To address these potential exposures, I include birth county fixed effects to absorb early life characteristics. Yet, it is nevertheless likely that segregation in the county at death net of birth county fixed effects still misses intervening exposures between birth and death that matter for health. Patterns of neighborhood attainment and residential mobility over the life course show that the modal neighborhood transitions are to places with similar characteristics \citep{sampson_neighborhood_2008}. In my sample, levels of segregation in 1940, which plausibly capture mid-life exposure, are positively and significantly correlated with levels of segregation in the county at death for both Blacks and Whites (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:corr_1940}). Moreover, across the life-course, migration propensity declines with age \citep{bernard_life-course_2014}: individuals are much less likely to migrate after age 40 and, when they do, it is usually short distances \citep{molloy_internal_2011,zimran_internal_2022}. For these reasons, I argue that the county of death plausibly represents a period of exposure encompassing mid-to-late life. To fully address the sensitivity of results to migration, I conduct subgroup analyses of the effects of segregation exposure in later life on samples where individuals (1) stayed in the same county all their life, (2) migrated to a new county, or (3) migrated and returned to their birth county.

To measure segregation, I use the dissimilarity index (D) \citep{duncan_methodological_1955}.
The dissimilarity index measures the evenness of the distribution of Black and White residents within a county, ranging from 0--100, and is interpreted as the share of Black or White individuals that would have to move to a different neighborhood for the racial composition of neighborhoods to be the same as the racial composition of the county.
The dissimilarity index is widely used in prior work \citep{hendi_where_2024,light_segregation_2019,massey_american_1993,cutler_are_1997} because it is straightforward to calculate, concordant with past empirical evidence, and has a concrete interpretation. The dissimilarity index for the county an individual resided in at the time of their death is calculated formally in Equation (1):

\begin{align} 
D = \frac 1 2 \sum_i^N | \frac{b_i}{B} - \frac{w_i}{W}| \times 100 
\end{align}

Where N is the number of census tracts in the county, $b_i$ and $w_i$ are the number of Black and White residents in the $i$th census tract, and B and W are the total number of Black and White residents in the county.
The dissimilarity scores are calculated from decennial Censuses of 1980, 1990, and 2000, which correspond to the year of death observed for sample members.\footnote{Although widely used and the interpretation convenient, D is well known to suffer from various index biases, outlined in \citet{fossett_new_2017} and others \citep{winship_revaluation_1977,carrington_measuring_1997}, particularly as they relate to temporal comparisons and variability in the population composition and relative group size of counties. Accordingly, in supplemental analyses, I replicate the main results with an adjusted D index that accounts for this bias and the results are robust to the adjustment (Appendix Tables A3--A5) \citep{fossett_new_2017}. In contrast to D, the bias-adjusted D measure is more complex to estimate and the interpretation less concrete \citep[p.~233]{fossett_new_2017}. For these reasons, I use the conventional dissimilarity index for main analyses.} 

\subsection{Railroad and Waterway Instrument Data}

I construct the instruments used in analyses from two sources of geospatial data.
First, I use Atack's historical GIS database of U.S. railroads, which digitizes the network of rail lines placed in operation between 1826 and 1911 \citeyearpar{atack_historical_2016}.
Retaining all segments in operation by 1911, I overlay the historical rail network on county boundaries and construct the Railroad Division Index (RDI) developed by \citet{ananat_wrong_2011}, which is described in detail in the Empirical Strategy section. I also compute an indicator for any pre-1911 railroad, total track length, and track density (kilometers of track per square kilometer of land area).

Second, I measure the arrangement of rivers and streams within counties using the U.S. Census Bureau's TIGER/Line linear hydrography shapefiles \citep{census_tiger_2023}, following their use in \citet{trounstine_segregation_2016}. From these files, I retain natural stream and river features and exclude man-made canals, ditches, and artificial connectors, which are endogenous to local development and settlement.

\subsection{Public Spending Data}

To measure county-level political-economic mechanisms, counties are linked to government finance records from the Government Finance Database (GFD), a compilation of Census of Governments and Annual Survey of Governments finance data \citep{pierson_government_2015}.
From the GFD, I extract the finances of county governments and construct measures of total tax revenue (including property and individual income tax components), health and hospital expenditures, Medicaid vendor payments, education spending, and direct welfare and cash assistance spending. Because government units are surveyed at varying frequencies, finance records are summed within each county-year and then averaged within the decade windows corresponding to the death decades observed in the sample (1980--1989, 1990--1999, and 2000--2005), so that each individual is assigned the policy environment of the decade in which they died. Each measure is expressed per county resident using decennial census population denominators. Dollar values are inflation adjusted to 2000 dollars. Appendix Table A1 provides descriptive statistics for the county-level policy measures used in the analyses.

\subsection{Analytic Sample}

My main analytic sample consists of ($N = 2{,}377{,}343$) individuals residing in $2{,}955$ counties at their time of death over three decades from 1980--2000. The resulting counties are filtered from all counties in the U.S. such that they have a Black population greater than 0 and coverage of the instruments.\footnote{The data filtering procedure and stepwise sample sizes are included in Table 3.} I also exclude individuals whose county of birth is not recorded, since migration between birth and death cannot be determined for them. The sibling sample, described further in the Empirical Strategy section, relies on a subset of the sample containing information on siblings in the CenSoc Numident.

Table 1 displays the individual and county characteristics of the 1905--1920 birth cohorts, separately for Black and White decedents, across the full data, the IV sample, and the flexible sibling sample. There is striking consistency in the observable characteristics of Black and White individuals across sample definitions, which provides support for the external validity of comparisons of estimates across samples.

\section{Empirical Strategy}
% =====================================================================

This section outlines the main analysis plan to estimate the causal effect of racial segregation on longevity. Existing studies provide compelling evidence of associations between segregation and poor health outcomes, but these studies are largely correlational. There are many features of places, such as infrastructure and environmental factors, that jointly determine segregation patterns and population health. At the individual level, as highlighted by early work \citep{massey_effect_1987}, segregation is theorized to be both a mechanism of racial stratification as well as an outcome of racial stratification that shapes the educational and economic status of individuals. Causal estimates of segregation are necessary, because correlational estimates cannot isolate the direct effects of segregation on health from confounding processes at both the macro and micro levels. 

To estimate the causal effects of segregation on longevity, I use a set of instrumental variables (IV) and a sibling fixed effects design. The logic of these instruments is to leverage quasi-random natural and historical variation in physical barriers that divide up counties to instrument for racial segregation. Greater spatial division of a county thus facilitates more segregation within places.\footnote{Similar instrumental variable designs have been used to examine the effect of segregation on educational outcomes \citep{cutler_are_1997,quillian_does_2014}, poverty and life expectancy \citep{ananat_wrong_2011,hendi_where_2024}, political composition \citep{ananat_segregation_2009}, and public spending \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016}.}
Instrumental variable designs are powerful because they offer one strategy to identify the effects of segregation in a setting where segregation patterns are otherwise impossible to experimentally manipulate. They also offer a high degree of geographic representativeness in the sample but with assumptions that can be hard to test. My approach in this paper is to triangulate the effect of segregation on longevity using a variety of complementary strategies with different assumptions and limitations, anchored by the IV estimates. I first describe the necessary IV assumptions and evidence in support of their validity, then I present a complementary instrument strategy and an alternative sibling fixed effects strategy.

IV designs rely on three core assumptions. First, relevance requires that the instruments strongly predict segregation \citep{angrist_one_2021}. Second, the exclusion restriction requires that instruments affect longevity only through segregation\footnote{Because there are more instruments than endogenous regressors, a natural test of the validity of many instruments is overidentification tests. These tests are commonly interpreted as testing the ``validity'' of the instruments, however overidentification tests must first assume that one instrument is valid (e.g., in the just-identified case) \citep{felton_handle_2024}, which is not informative for assessing validity. A more informative interpretation from \citet{mogstad_causal_2021} is that rejection of overidentification tests suggests that there is heterogeneity in treatment effects across subpopulations affected by the different instruments, which is not in reference to validity. Additionally, overidentification tests assume constant treatment effects, which is unreasonable in a setting where the IV estimate is a local average treatment effect. As expected, the instruments generally fail to pass these tests given the large sample size of my data, where observations are in the millions. An alternative to overidentification tests is placebo analyses that require a researcher find an additional instrument that shares all the same confounders as the original instrument but is thought to not affect the treatment \citep{felton_handle_2024}. While informative, the shared confounders assumption of placebo instruments is also quite strong and the search for a candidate placebo instrument faces the same challenges as the search for a credible instrument.}. Third, monotonicity requires that the instruments push segregation in the same direction for all units. While a direct test of this assumption is not possible, I examine whether first-stage instrument strength declines over time in the way monotonicity would predict, and discuss the evidence in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:iv_monotonicity}. Conditional on these assumptions, IV estimates are interpreted as the local average treatment effect (LATE) \citep{imbens_identification_1994}, or the treatment effect among those living in counties where segregation increased because of greater physical division.

The preferred instrumental variable strategy is the railroad division index (RDI) proposed by \citet{ananat_wrong_2011}. This instrument is constructed for counties\footnote{This has been used previously in \citet{quillian_does_2014,ananat_segregation_2009,chyn_effects_2026} at the metro level.} using the historical railroad shapefiles described above \citep{atack_historical_2016}. The RDI measures the extent to which counties are divided up by intersections with historical railroad lines that were established in the early 20th Century. The RDI calculates the number of intersections that a county has with rail lines in a standardized index that ranges from 0 to 1, with higher values indicating more division. In addition to rail lines, I also instrument with the density of railroad tracks per square kilometer of the county. The calculation of the RDI is described as follows: 

\begin{align} 
RDI = 1 - \sum_i (a_i/A)^2
\end{align}

 Where $a_i$ is the area of each subpolygon created by cutting the county with the rail network and $A$ is the total county area. The index equals 0 for counties left undivided by railroads and approaches 1 as rail lines subdivide a county into many small pieces, capturing the physical partitioning of places that facilitated racially divided neighborhood boundaries.

The intuition behind the RDI is that historical railroads provided natural barriers within places that populations could cluster around, thus creating higher levels of baseline segregation. \citet{ananat_wrong_2011} demonstrates that historical White segregationist efforts often utilized natural barriers such as rail lines to establish communities and neighborhoods, because these barriers are naturally difficult to navigate; crucially, she shows that the RDI, while predictive of segregation, does not predict other place-level characteristics.\footnote{In its original usage \citep{ananat_wrong_2011}, the RDI is constructed for Northern metropolitan areas, because the majority of African Americans lived almost entirely in the South before the 1900s when the railroads were laid. Therefore only in non-Southern cities can railroad configuration be treated as exogenous to race. Thus the logic of exclusion is buttressed by this claim, however this limits the geographic generalizability of the estimates, despite the fact that large Black populations continued to reside in the South and may differ in important ways. To overcome this, I adjust for residence in the South in all models and test the robustness of these results to potential bias using alternative instruments that do not rely on the same geographic limitations and sibling fixed effects estimates that do not require exclusion restrictions.}

There are two leading issues that may undermine exclusion. First, while railroad division does not directly relate to railroad \textit{exposure} per se, it could be indirectly related. These instruments use \textit{configuration} of barriers within a county rather than exposure to the barriers themselves. Despite the exposure-division distinction, configuration could violate exclusion. The exclusion restriction hinges on the assumption that early 20th Century railroads do not affect mortality directly. Railroads could affect mortality by generating financial prosperity in areas surrounding tracks which could promote better health. \citet{black_impact_2015}, using railroad proximity to instrument for migration, show that railroads had little association with economic outcomes for a non-selective group of Southern-born White older adults, indicating that the long-term health impacts of railroad induced prosperity are likely minimal. To test this directly, I regress education and income of the 1905--1920 birth cohorts in 1940 on the RDI to test whether railroad division is directly related to socioeconomic outcomes. I estimate these models for those that did not migrate during their lifetime to capture the potential impact of the RDI for those exposed continuously to the same place\footnote{Non-migration defined simply by comparing county of birth with county of death leaves open whether an individual remained in that county throughout the intervening decades. The 1940 Census supplies one intermediate observation with which to check this. Among those who died in their birth county, $32$ percent of Blacks and $16$ percent of Whites were living in a different county in 1940, and so are return migrants rather than lifelong residents. Appendix Table A6 reports the full pattern of agreement among birth, 1940, and death county. To avoid misclassifying these return migrants as non-migrants, Appendix Table A7 therefore defines non-migration under the stricter criterion that additionally requires residence in the same county at the 1940 Census. Note also that exclusion need only hold for the county-at-death exposure, which is the period of exposure where segregation is calculated.} (Appendix Table A7). Among those who did not migrate during their lifetime (i.e., those for whom I can confidently attribute the RDI value to birth place, 1940 residence, as well as death place), education is unrelated to the RDI for Blacks but slightly positive and significant for Whites. The RDI is not associated with income for Blacks but is slightly positive for Whites. Two features of these estimates limit their bearing on the exclusion restriction. First, the magnitudes are small. Because the RDI ranges from 0 to 1, the White education coefficient implies that moving across the entire range of the instrument corresponds to roughly $0.23$ years, or under three months, of additional schooling. Second, and more consequentially, the sign of these effects suggests that their impact on the main estimates is conservative. Education and income both raise longevity, so a positive association between the RDI and 1940 socioeconomic status should bias the instrumental variable estimates toward zero. Whatever residual violation these coefficients represent, it would attenuate the main estimates rather than upwardly bias them.  

Second, railroads could also result in increased hazards such as pollution that harm health \citep{dockery_association_1993}. In particular, small particulate matter in the air resulting from exhaust and emissions is associated with poor health outcomes \citep{sangkham_update_2024}. To test the extent to which the RDI could directly affect health through pollution, I examine the relationship between the RDI and small Particulate Matter (PM2.5) concentration $PM2.5/m^3$ and railroad division in U.S. counties in 1999 and 2000.\footnote{Prior to the 1990s, the Environmental Protection Agency did not collect data on PM2.5 levels. Modeled estimates are available for earlier periods, but may be subject to bias.} PM2.5 measures the concentration of tiny dust and soot particles in the air in micrograms per cubic meter ($\mu /m^3$). The RDI and PM2.5 concentration are positively and significantly associated, but the magnitude is very small (Appendix Table A8). Going from a county that has no railroad division to one that is completely divided by rail lines represents a difference in PM2.5 concentration of $1.76$. \citet{pope_lung_2002} found that a 10-point increase in PM2.5 concentration was associated with up to an 8 percent increase in risk of mortality. This suggests that the PM2.5 concentration generated by going from a county with no railroad division to complete railroad division would be approximately a $1.4$ percent increase in risk, which suggests that the direct effects on mortality are potentially minimal.

Despite evidence presented against exclusion violations owing to direct effects of the RDI on health, it is nevertheless possible that there is still bias present. The second set of instruments employed to complement this strategy utilize the density of natural rivers and streams within a county.\footnote{Each of the instruments relying on spatial division will be correlated to some degree, as demonstrated in Appendix Table A9, however the overall $R^2$ between instruments is relatively weak. The pairwise $R^2$ between the RDI and rivers is $0.002$. These relatively low values indicate that each of these instruments is picking up on a different dimension of spatial division in an area rather than substituting for another measure.} For each county, I compute the number of distinct named rivers and streams, along with total stream length and stream density \citep{trounstine_segregation_2016}.\footnote{Man made rivers and streams are omitted.}

The logic of using natural streams and rivers to instrument for segregation relies on the quasi-randomness of these natural boundaries. Like the RDI, rivers and streams divide up areas in ways that shape population settlement patterns around them but are plausibly unrelated to contemporary outcomes. While railroads and highways have been used historically to segregate, those boundaries are man-made, which raises the concern that they could be endogenous to segregation in some way. Natural boundaries are not subject to these sources of bias because they are difficult for humans to manipulate. Rivers and streams also overcome the issues posed by generalizing the RDI to the South, because they do not rely on any particular history of settlement. It is still possible, however, that more rivers and streams are correlated with greater availability of green spaces and natural areas that carry health benefits, thus violating the exclusion restriction. 

Thus far, different instrument strategies carry the shared concerns that geographic arrangements could affect health in residual ways. To complement these IV approaches, I utilize a sibling fixed effects identification strategy that does not rely on any of these exclusion restrictions.\footnote{Sibling fixed effects strategies have been used in prior literature to estimate the effects of education on mortality \citep{halpern-manners_effects_2020} and the effects of neighborhoods on intergenerational mobility \citep{chetty_impacts_2018}.} Sibling designs identify effects by controlling for unobserved heterogeneity at the family-level by differencing out shared factors common among siblings (e.g., family wealth, health endowments, genetic factors, etc.). After controlling for individual demographic characteristics that vary across siblings, these designs identify the effect of segregation on longevity from differences in siblings' residential environments.

The sibling fixed effects strategy is advantageous in this study context, because it relies on different assumptions and sources of identifying variation to establish a causal relationship. Sibling fixed effects also carry important considerations regarding external validity, due to their reliance on a subset of matched sibling pairs and discordance in residential environments among siblings. Fortunately, observable characteristics of the sibling sample track the full sample closely (as shown in Table 1), providing confidence in their representativeness compared to the broader population.

Each of these designs relies on different identifying variation and assumptions for identifying causal effects. Because sibling designs do not require the same exclusion restriction assumptions as the instruments, agreement across designs suggests that exclusion violations do not drive results, which allows for the triangulation of the effect of segregation on longevity.

\subsection{Estimation Strategy}

The estimation strategy in this paper revolves around three sets of models stratified by race: OLS regression models, Instrumental Variable regression models, and sibling fixed effects models. First, I estimate a set of OLS regression models of the form: 

\begin{align} 
&\text{(OLS)} \quad Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta Segregation_{i} +  \pmb{\beta} X_i + \gamma_{b(i)} + \lambda_{c(i)} + \epsilon_i 
\end{align}

Where $\beta segregation$ is the dissimilarity in an individual’s county at death. In all models, segregation is expressed on a scale from $0$--$100$ so that the estimate corresponds to each unit increase in dissimilarity rather than the contrast of a perfectly integrated or segregated city. $\pmb{\beta}$ is a vector of regression coefficients and $X$ is a vector of individual-level control variables and $\gamma$ and $\lambda$ are fixed effects for individual birth year and birth county.

Second, I estimate a set of Instrumental Variable regressions via two-stage least squares estimation according to the following form: 

\begin{align}
&\text{(First-Stage)} \quad \beta Segregation_{i} = instrument \in
\begin{cases}
\small RDI + Rail\ Length/km^2, \\
N\ Waterways + Waterway\ Length/km^2
\end{cases} & \\
&\text{(IV)} \qquad\quad\ \ Y_i = \beta_0 + \beta Segregation_{i} + \pmb{\beta} X_i + \gamma_{b(i)} + \lambda_{c(i)} + \epsilon_i & \nonumber
\end{align}

Where the form of the model is the same as OLS, except that segregation has now been instrumented by one of the instrument sets. In contrast to regression models estimated by OLS, the coefficient on segregation changes to the local average treatment effect of segregation on the longevity among compliers: those whose counties became more segregated in response to a denser configuration of physical barriers.

Third, I estimate the sibling fixed effects models on the subsample of sibling data using the same regression specification as equation (3), except that an identifier grouping individuals into siblings within families is added. The models are estimated as follows: 

\begin{align} 
&\text{(Sibling FE)} \ Y_{ij} = \beta_0 + \beta Segregation_{i} +  \pmb{\beta} X_i + \gamma_{b(i)} + \lambda_{c(i)} + \sigma_{d(j)} + \epsilon_i 
\end{align}

Where $\sigma_{d(j)}$ is a family fixed effect and the indexing of the model has been adapted to index individuals $i$ within families $j$. 

In all models, I control for a rich set of individual and contextual covariates to adjust for any residual confounding.
In fully specified models, I control for sex, education, marital status, occupation, whether an individual migrated between birth and death, whether they reside in the South, urban-rural fixed effects\footnote{Between 1993 and 2003, Rural-Urban Continuum codes changed from their definitions between 1983 and 1993. For the 1983--1993 codes, metro counties were classified using a scheme ranging from 0--3, with counties belonging to a metro area of 1 million or more categorized into Central and Fringe counties. In 2003, this scheme was revised so that counties were classified based only by population, with Fringe and Central collapsed into one category. Accordingly, to increase comparability, I collapse codes 0 and 1 in the 1983 and 1993 continuum code schemes into one category so that they are harmonized to the 1--3 scheme used in 2003.}, and birth year and state fixed effects. 

It is important to note the multi-level structure of these data where segregation is measured at the county level, while individuals are clustered within counties. Because the regressor of interest is 
constant within cluster and errors are plausibly correlated within cluster, default 
standard errors are potentially severely downward-biased \citep{moulton_illustration_1990, 
cameron_practitioners_2015}. In all models I report cluster-robust standard 
errors at the county level.\footnote{This addresses the same source of dependence that a 
random-intercept multilevel model would absorb, but does so without assuming the within-cluster error 
covariance structure be correctly specified, which are necessary for the multilevel estimator's efficiency and inferential validity to hold \citep{cameron_practitioners_2015}. Since my interest is in the county-level association rather than in variance decomposition, the clustering solution is preferred because it imposes fewer assumptions on the error process.}

\section{Results}
% =====================================================================
I present the main results in the order described in the Empirical Strategy. Tables of regression coefficients used to produce all figures are reported in Section~\ref{sec:tabfig}. I begin with the relevance of the instruments, because their credibility rests on this assumption. I then report the OLS associations between segregation and longevity separately for Black and White individuals, which establish the sign and magnitude of the relationship in the dataset. I then turn to the instrumental variable estimates and sibling fixed effects estimates to triangulate the effects of segregation on longevity across empirical strategies. Throughout, I estimate all models separately by race and express the estimates as the effect of a 10-point increase in the dissimilarity index.

First, the relevance assumption requires that the instruments strongly predict segregation. Figure \ref{fig:first-stage} displays binned scatterplots of the first-stage relationship between each instrument and county dissimilarity, residualized on the remaining instrument in each strategy.\footnote{The binscatter plot is a method to visualize bivariate relationships in large datasets. Because there are $2{,}955$ unique counties, examining a scatterplot with individual points is visually difficult. The binscatter approach bins the distribution of the independent variable and then takes the average of the outcome variable in each bin. These binned $y-x$ relationships are then visualized in a scatterplot to approximate the relationship. Full regression results are reported in Table 4.} Panel A shows that counties more granularly divided by the pre-1911 railroad network are substantially more segregated. For example, a county at the top of the RDI distribution is roughly $7$--$8$ points more segregated than an undivided county. Consistent with the relevance assumption, the first-stage $F$-statistic of $131.19$ far exceeds conventional thresholds for instrument strength. Panel B shows the analogous relationship for the natural-boundary strategy, where counties containing more named rivers are more segregated, with a first-stage $F$-statistic of $38.21$. The instruments are therefore strong predictors of segregation in both samples, and the two strategies recover first stages of similar magnitude despite drawing on entirely different sources of spatial division.

\subsection{Main Estimates of Segregation on Longevity}

Next, I present main estimates of the OLS association between segregation and longevity across samples by race and the IV and sibling fixed effects estimates. Figure \ref{fig:ols-all} reports OLS associations between segregation and longevity, estimated separately for Black and White decedents with birth year, birth state, and urban-rural fixed effects, and with the addition of individual controls and occupation fixed effects. Because the samples used by the IV and sibling fixed effects strategy differ, I re-estimate the OLS association on each of the three estimation samples that the subsequent models use. The association is consistently negative for both groups regardless of the sample considered and with the inclusion of controls. Among Black individuals, a 10-point increase in dissimilarity is associated with $0.72$ fewer years of life; among White individuals, the same increase is associated with $0.64$ fewer years. The Black estimates are less precise in the sibling samples, which is expected given that the sibling subsamples contain between $3{,}300$ and $4{,}578$ Black individuals, depending on the matching procedure, compared with $123{,}038$ in the IV sample. Whatever variation in sample composition the identification strategies introduce, it does not by itself move the association. 

Recall that Figure \ref{fig:zero-sum-processes} described three stylized race-specific relationships between segregation and longevity that are consistent with Black-White disparities. These OLS associations provide initial support in favor of interpretation (A), mutual harm from segregation, rather than a zero-sum relationship, which would require a positive coefficient for Whites. The coefficients are negative for both Blacks and Whites in every sample, which is the pattern Panel (A) predicts and is inconsistent with the White advantage that Panel (B) implies. These associations are nevertheless descriptive. Thus, I turn to the instrumental variable and sibling fixed effects estimates to recover the causal effect.

\begin{figure}[h!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../FigTab/ols_estimates_all.jpeg}
\caption{OLS Estimates of the Association Between Segregation and Longevity, by Race and Estimation Sample}
\label{fig:ols-all}
\end{figure}

Each of these identification strategies carries its own strengths and weaknesses, ranging from the testability of the assumptions to external validity, but their value lies in comparison with one another. The IV designs recover a local average treatment effect among compliers and depend on an exclusion restriction that cannot be tested directly; the sibling fixed effects design requires no exclusion restriction. Figure \ref{fig:iv-all} assembles fully-adjusted estimates from all four strategies. The regression tables and unadjusted estimates are presented in Section \ref{sec:tabfig}.

Consistent with the OLS associations, segregation reduces the longevity of both Black and White Americans. Using the RDI instrument in the fully specified models, a 10-point increase in dissimilarity reduces Black longevity by $0.57$ years (95\% CI: $-0.72$, $-0.43$) and White longevity by $0.44$ years (95\% CI: $-0.57$, $-0.32$). The rivers instrument yields estimates of $0.81$ fewer years for Black individuals (95\% CI: $-1.38$, $-0.23$) and $0.56$ fewer years for White individuals (95\% CI: $-1.04$, $-0.08$). The rivers estimates are far noisier than the RDI estimates, with standard errors roughly four times as large for both groups, and their confidence intervals span most of the range of magnitudes the other strategies recover. What the two instruments share is the conclusion that segregation reduces longevity for both groups. Taken together, a 10-point increase in segregation costs Black Americans between roughly seven and ten months of life across the two instruments, and White Americans between roughly five and seven months.\footnote{Because all models are estimated separately by race, the Black and White coefficients within any single specification are not directly comparable, and I discuss the full set of strategies later in this section.}

The results using sibling fixed effects models confirm these patterns. In the exact-match sample, segregation reduces Black longevity by $0.94$ years (95\% CI: $-1.22$, $-0.67$) and White longevity by $0.76$ years (95\% CI: $-0.83$, $-0.70$). The flexible-match sample gives almost identical figures, at $0.94$ years for Black decedents (95\% CI: $-1.18$, $-0.71$) and $0.78$ years for White decedents (95\% CI: $-0.85$, $-0.71$).

\begin{figure}[h!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../FigTab/iv_plot_all.jpeg}
\caption{Estimates of the Effect of Segregation on Longevity Across Identification Strategies, by Race}
\label{fig:iv-all}
\end{figure}

Across identification strategies, estimates reveal inequalities in the magnitude of the effects of segregation on longevity by race that are obscured by OLS associations. The consistent takeaway across models is that higher segregation reduces longevity for Black and White Americans alike, with some variability in magnitude. Taking the strengths and weaknesses of each design into account, the triangulation that consistently points toward the same sign and ordering of race-specific effects gives confidence in a causal interpretation.

Under fully-adjusted RDI estimates, a 10-point increase in dissimilarity widens the Black-White longevity gap by $0.13$ years. The gap and the individual-level regressions tell different stories about ostensibly the same relationship. This longevity gap arises not from a zero-sum process, but from lowering the longevity of both groups. A naive analysis would recover a Black disadvantage of about a month and a half, while missing the context that both groups are disadvantaged by segregation.\footnote{Additional analyses in Section \ref{sec:append_ed} examine how the effects of segregation on longevity vary for Blacks and Whites. While education does not moderate the effects of segregation for Blacks, there is evidence of an education gradient for Whites. Higher-educated Whites, though still harmed by segregation, are buffered in the extent to which segregation affects longevity. These conclusions are largely consistent with the account in \citet{dubois_black_1935}, which argues that working-class Whites are harmed the most by racial stratification.}

It is worth noting that the magnitude of estimates differ across strategies. The sibling fixed effects estimates are larger in magnitude relative to OLS and the RDI estimates are attenuated relative to OLS.\footnote{The rivers estimates are also larger than OLS, but they are far noisier, which suggests that the point estimate is larger in magnitude but very imprecise.} These differences are expected for a few reasons. Each design targets a different estimand. OLS recovers a population association while the IV estimates recover the LATE, which refers to estimates for the population of compliers who live in counties that became more segregated in response to the instruments. The sibling fixed effects models identify the effects of segregation based on differences in county of residence of siblings. Regarding the sibling fixed effects estimates, families with greater resources and better health may plausibly have sorted into large, highly segregated counties. Therefore, family fixed effects absorb this selection, consistent with the sibling estimates becoming larger. The RDI estimates being smaller than OLS is consistent with the possibility that individuals' health and racial sorting patterns are determined by a common cause. Differences in magnitude notwithstanding, the sign and the race-specific ordering of the effects yield the same empirical conclusions about the relationship between segregation and longevity disparities.

A natural extension to these results is how they vary by migration history. Because the birth cohorts considered in this paper were alive during the Great Migration that saw unprecedented numbers of Black Americans move from the South to the North, it is possible that there is important heterogeneity or bias induced by migration status. Individuals who migrate may be positively selected on health and longevity, which could bias the estimates of segregation on longevity. Segregation exposure in the county at death also serves as a noisy proxy for lifetime residential exposure which induces measurement error; this can be seen in Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:corr_1940}, which shows the correlation between segregation in the county of residence in 1940 and county at death is positive and significant, but not perfectly predictive. This noise would suggest that the effects of segregation are largest for those continuously exposed to the same county and smaller for those who migrate \citep{card_earnings_1994}. To assess these patterns empirically, I stratify the sample by three-way migration status: non-movers (those who lived in the same county at birth and death), return migrants (those who lived in a different county at birth and death but returned to their birth county), and movers (those who lived in a different county at birth and death). Figure \ref{fig:iv_mig3} (Table 9) presents the main RDI IV estimates of the effect of segregation on longevity by migration status for each race.\footnote{The alternative strategies are not presented stratified by migration status because of limited variation within families partitioned by migration status and limited instrument strength for the rivers IV. First, sample splits are infeasible for the sibling fixed effects design because partitions by migration status leave nearly every family with one member, which the sibling FE removes as a singleton. Second, the full sample instrument strength of the rivers instrument is weaker than the RDI. When partitioned by subsample, the first-stage power of the rivers IV is far lower and therefore too weak to be informative.} 

\begin{figure}[h!tbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{../FigTab/mig3_estimates.jpeg}
\caption{Estimates of the Effect of Segregation on Longevity by Migration Status, by Race}
\label{fig:iv_mig3}
\end{figure}

The estimates are negative for all groups, with the largest effects observed for non-movers and return migrants, and the smallest effects for movers. These results indicate that while there is important heterogeneity by migration status, the overall relationship between segregation and longevity is consistently negative across subgroups.

How should one understand the magnitude of these effects substantively? In the context of contemporary demographic research on the social determinants of longevity, these effects are substantial. Consider the effect of years of education. \citet{halpern-manners_effects_2020} reports that each additional year of education increases longevity by $.34$ years using sibling fixed effects models and the 1940 full count Census. In comparison the RDI estimates suggest that the effect of increasing segregation by 10 points is of similar magnitude to the effects of attaining roughly $1.3$ to $1.7$ additional years of education. Though these estimates are quantitatively large, it is important to situate this 10-point contrast within the change in segregation over time. In sample counties in 1980 the mean dissimilarity was $46.14$ and by 2000 it was $35.90$, a $10.24$ point decline. In other words, the 10-point change represents the effect of moving from the average segregated county in 1980 to the average segregated county in 2000.

\subsection{Political-Economic Mechanisms of Segregation}

What explains the effects of segregation on longevity for White and Black Americans? In this paper, I argue that political-economic mechanisms play an important role in shaping the outcomes of individuals because of their macro-level pathways tied directly to health and wellbeing. To test the importance of several of these mechanisms, I analyze how these mechanisms shift coefficients in adjusted RDI IV regressions.\footnote{A descriptive table on the segregation mechanisms tested here is displayed in Appendix Table A1.} I start with estimates using the RDI stratified by race and include each set of mechanisms which are then compared to the baseline adjusted model. 

The logic of the exercise is the following. If segregation shortens lives partly by reshaping the fiscal and redistributive capacity of the places people live in, then conditioning on those policy channels should absorb part of the reduced-form effect recovered in the previous section. If instead the effect operates entirely through channels unrelated to local political economy, adding these controls should leave the coefficient on $D$ unchanged. I consider four county-level channels measured over the 1980, 1990, and 2000 decennial panels: total taxes per capita, direct welfare expenditure per capita, health and hospital expenditure per capita, and Medicaid vendor payments per capita, all constructed from county government finance records. Each model adds one channel at a time to the fully-adjusted RDI specification with a final specification fully adjusting for all policy variables, and all models are re-estimated on the subsample of counties with non-missing policy data ($100{,}743$ Black and $2{,}069{,}371$ White decedents) so that the baseline and the mechanism models are directly comparable.

\input{../FigTab/mechanism_ols_iv_table.tex}

Table 2 displays OLS and IV regression estimates of the aggregate relationship between racial segregation and public spending outcomes. In the IV specification, racial segregation is negatively associated with tax revenue, health spending, and welfare spending, but does not appear to be related to Medicaid spending. Both specifications produce estimates with the same sign, with the exception of the estimate for welfare, which flips sign. However, it is important to note that the estimates of the relationship between segregation and welfare are noisy.

Figure \ref{fig:mechanisms} reports estimates of the effect of segregation on longevity by race after adding each of the mechanisms to individual-level regression models. Each estimate is the coefficient on $D$ from the fully-adjusted RDI IV model, and the label to the right of each interval expresses it as a percentage of that race's baseline estimate, in which no mechanism is controlled. Values below 100 percent indicate that the channel absorbs part of the effect of segregation on longevity; values at or above 100 percent indicate that it absorbs none of it. All percentages reported in this section are computed from the unrounded coefficients and so will not reproduce exactly from the three-decimal entries in Table 10.

Because these models are restricted to counties with non-missing policy data, their baseline estimates are slightly smaller than the headline RDI estimates reported above: a 10-point increase in dissimilarity reduces longevity by $0.52$ years for Black decedents and $0.38$ years for White decedents on this subsample. All percentages below are expressed relative to these subsample baselines rather than to the full-sample estimates.

First, fiscal variables absorb a non-trivial share of the effect for both Blacks and Whites. Controlling for county taxes per capita reduces the coefficient on segregation to $81.3$ percent of its baseline value among Black decedents and to $89.4$ percent among White decedents. The tax base is also the mechanism that absorbs a similar share of the total effect for both groups, consistent with theory about their broad-based effects. In aggregate, segregation sharply reduces the tax base: the IV estimate in Table 2 implies roughly \$81 less tax revenue per resident for every 10-point increase in dissimilarity.\footnote{Unlike the individual-level longevity models, which scale $D$ from $0$ to $100$, the county-level policy models in Table 2 scale $D$ from $0$ to $1$. The coefficient of $-812.2$ is therefore the contrast between a perfectly integrated and a perfectly segregated county; dividing by ten expresses it on the 10-point scale used throughout the rest of the paper.}

Second, direct welfare expenditure is asymmetric by race. It absorbs about a quarter of the Black estimate ($76.4$ percent) and about a tenth of the White estimate ($89.0$ percent) --- for Black decedents it is the single largest channel of the four. The racial asymmetry in these relationships is not surprising considering the large proportions of the Black population receiving welfare relative to Whites. The consequence of this asymmetry is that most of the racial difference in total mediated share ($34.1$ versus $17.8$ percent, a difference of $16.3$ points) is attributable to how welfare absorbs the effects of segregation differentially by race: welfare alone accounts for $12.6$ of those $16.3$ points. Thus, because segregation reduces welfare spending, this differentially harms Black residents above and beyond the general fiscal contraction that harms both groups.

Third, health and hospital expenditures barely shift the estimates for either group ($98.1$ percent for Black decedents); for Whites the coefficient is marginally larger than baseline once health spending is included ($101.6$ percent). Medicaid vendor payments absorb slightly less than a tenth of the effect for either group ($91.6$ and $91.7$ percent).

When considering policy portfolios of places, these joint mechanisms account for $34.1$ percent of the estimate of segregation on longevity for Blacks and $17.8$ percent of the estimate for Whites. Taxation absorbs $18.7$ percent of the Black estimate and $10.6$ percent of the White estimate, and welfare absorbs $23.6$ and $11.0$ percent respectively. For both groups the joint specification absorbs more than any channel does alone, so the channels are substantially non-overlapping rather than proxies for one another. In the context of prior work, the explanatory power of these four policy mechanisms is notable; \citet{popescu_racial_2018} for instance found that 45\% of the relationship between metropolitan segregation and life expectancy was explained by the joint contribution of income, poverty, education, employment, and female household shares. In other words, policy mechanisms alone account for $40$ to $76$ percent, for White and Black effects respectively, of the total contribution of socioeconomic factors reported in \citet{popescu_racial_2018}. What is clear for both groups is that the general fiscal capacity of places, rather than any single expenditure category, plays a substantial role in attenuating the effects of segregation on health. 

These estimates should be read as bounds on mediated shares rather than as point estimates. The policy channels are measured post-treatment, so conditioning on them can introduce bias from intermediate confounders that jointly determine local policy and longevity, and the confidence intervals on the mechanism-adjusted coefficients overlap with the baseline coefficient. Nevertheless, this exercise supports the claim that the political economy of segregated places is a substantial explanatory pathway relating segregation to outcomes. These political economy pathways account for about $18$ percent of the longevity penalty for White Americans and $34$ percent of the penalty for Black Americans. These estimates are consistent with the argument that segregation affects places in ways that extend beyond individual neighborhood contexts.

\subsection{Double Truncation and Mortality Crossover}
\label{sec:truncation}

Thus far the estimates presented suggest that segregation had significant, negative effects on the longevity of both Black and White Americans aged 65 and older. However, because surviving to 65 years and older is selective, this raises the possibility that race-specific processes of mortality selection could bias these estimates. A large literature in demography focused on the descriptively named ``Black-White mortality crossover'' has found that at advanced ages, positively selected Black older adults have lower mortality risk compared to White older adults of the same age. In this setting, it is possible that the magnitude of the negative estimates for Whites could be accounted for by this selection and the magnitude of the estimates for Blacks could be a significant underestimate of the full magnitude.

\begin{figure}[h!tbp]
\centering 
\includegraphics[width=.75\textwidth]{../FigTab/gompertz_figure.jpeg}
\caption{Double-Truncation-Corrected Estimates of the Relationship Between Segregation and Longevity}
\label{fig:gompertz}
\end{figure}

Literature on the Black-White mortality crossover generally finds that the crossover occurs at advanced ages. Prior scholarship has generally found that the crossover occurs between the mid- and late 80s (see \citealp{breen_black_2026} for a review). Because the majority of this sample is between the ages of 67 and 82, bias from the crossover plausibly affects only the oldest members of the sample. To provide a quantification of the bias induced by truncated data, \citet{goldstein_mortality_2023} developed a Gompertz maximum likelihood estimation procedure to model partially observed cohorts while explicitly accounting for truncation. The Gompertz procedure takes into account the truncated nature of the data and re-estimates the associations recovered by a traditional regression model using a log of the Gompertz distribution to correct estimates. This allows for a more accurate estimation of longevity and characterization of group-differences in longevity, which allows a quantification of the potential for the mortality crossover to bias estimates directly.

Figure \ref{fig:gompertz} provides a graphical representation of my main regression estimates by race in comparison to the Gompertz-corrected estimates. The Gompertz estimates are obtained by modeling death age as a function of segregation, birth year, birth state, and urban-rural fixed effects. This estimate is then paired with an identical OLS regression of the same form for a direct comparison of the difference between estimates. The bias induced by double truncation is minimal in magnitude and results reach the same conclusions as the main estimates. The Gompertz-corrected estimate for Black longevity is $7$ percent larger than the regression-based estimate and the White longevity estimate is $6$ percent larger. This increase in the magnitude is consistent with prior scholarship that estimates with truncated data are biased toward zero. These corrected estimates are thus slightly larger, but recover the same substantive Black-White longevity gap estimated via race-stratified regression models.

\section{Discussion}
% =====================================================================

Racial segregation is an important mechanism of racial stratification that affects the health and wellbeing of Black populations. At the same time, popular and scholarly discourse hypothesizes that racial segregation generates Black-White inequality by advantaging Whites while simultaneously disadvantaging Blacks. Despite the ubiquity of this explanation, Black-White disparities are consistent with multiple distinct processes that do not require White advantage. Namely, segregation could harm Whites as well or could have no effect on their outcomes. While most of the literature on segregation emphasizes the role of neighborhoods as mechanisms, a burgeoning interdisciplinary literature has documented how racial segregation shapes the political economy of places in profound ways by reducing spending on public goods that have implications for all residents. In this paper, I first test the argument that racial segregation creates Black-White disparities by advantaging Whites by examining the effect of racial segregation on the longevity of Black and White Americans using complete count 1940 Census data and several complementary identification strategies. I then use county-level government finance data to explore how much political-economic mechanisms contribute to explaining segregation effects. 

I find that, contrary to the White advantage hypothesis, racial segregation reduces the longevity of both Black and White Americans. A 10-point increase in segregation reduced Black longevity by $0.57$ years and White longevity by $0.44$ years in the RDI IV specification. Across various complementary specifications and subgroups (Appendix Tables A2--A5 and A10--A15), the effects of segregation are consistently negative for Blacks and Whites. These effects indicate that segregation costs White and Black individuals substantially and question the centrality of neighborhood-level accounts of segregation. To empirically test the scope for macro-level, political-economic mechanisms, I show that these effects are partially mediated by public spending and tax base pathways. Together, public spending accounts for $34.1$ percent of the effect for Black Americans and $17.8$ percent for White Americans. Taxation and welfare spending are the two substantial channels, absorbing $18.7$ and $23.6$ percent of the Black estimate and $10.6$ and $11.0$ percent of the White estimate. The contribution of spending diverges by race most sharply for welfare, which absorbs more than twice as large a share of the Black estimate as of the White one.

This study makes several new contributions to the literature on racial segregation and racial inequality. I show that racial segregation reduces Black and White longevity, which produces Black-White disparities not by advantaging Whites but through mutual, but asymmetric harm. While segregation is commonly understood to be an advantaging mechanism for Whites, these results show consistently that segregation, and by extension structural racism \citep{louie_fear_2024}, harms both groups across a variety of identification strategies, specifications using weights and alternative measures of segregation, and robustly manifests within subgroups by birth cohort, gender, and military status (see Appendix). Black-White mortality disparities are a large and enduring differentiator of the life chances of racial groups. In 2020, the Black-White life-expectancy gap at 65 years old was $2.4$ years \citep{arias_united_2022}. These estimates imply that desegregating the U.S. by 10 points would reduce the life-expectancy gap at 65 years to $2.27$ years, an approximately $1.56$ month reduction.\footnote{This number is calculated by adding the race-specific estimate of a 10-point increase in dissimilarity from the main RDI specification to the estimated Black and White life expectancies. The 10-point figure is approximately the decline in segregation observed in sample counties between 1980--2000. $\delta = (W = (18.6 + .44)) - (B = (16.2 + .57)) = 2.27$.} Although the effects of segregation are unequal in magnitude for Whites and Blacks, these estimates suggest that declines in racial segregation translated to increases in both Black and White longevity rather than reducing White advantages. 

I contribute to the literature on racial segregation by studying mechanisms of segregation that occur beyond neighborhoods. While White and Black individuals are differentially sorted into different neighborhoods within places, they are subject to the same politics and policy portfolios of places that operate beyond neighborhoods. Inequalities in the tax base contribute substantially to explaining effects for both groups at similar magnitudes, while welfare spending explains only Black effects. This may be because welfare receipt is already sharply patterned by race, so the stratified effects of inequalities in welfare spending induced by segregation are magnified when they interact with existing inequalities, while tax base inequalities are more evenly distributed because they are not contingent on individual take-up. In contrast to prior studies regarding crime and victimization \citep{light_segregation_2019} that provide clear evidence for White advantage and studies of socioeconomic outcomes that are more varied \citep{quillian_does_2014,cutler_are_1997}, these results suggest that the zero-sum potential for segregation may be confined to outcomes that are spatially contingent (e.g., proximity to violence) rather than influenced by several contexts. The importance of county-level mechanisms in explaining effects for both groups strengthens this conclusion.

Theories of racial segregation and racial stratification that explain its maintenance through White benefit imply material gains for Whites. These estimates show that the benefits of segregation for Whites, if any, are largely symbolic rather than material. This raises questions about the mechanisms that generate perceived benefits without material gains. A historical answer to this question can be found in \citet{dubois_black_1935}, which situates the symbolic benefits of racial stratification, the wages of Whiteness, as a critical mechanism that allowed Southern elites to maintain political and economic power in the South. In this way, segregation can be seen as an instrument of larger political and economic power structures. Within this framework, White resistance to integration despite material disadvantages plays a key role in maintaining segregated arrangements. However, individual preferences for segregation are only one part of a larger constellation of actors and mechanisms that reinforce segregated arrangements in contemporary periods \citep{krysan_cycle_2017}. Thus, these results raise new questions for future research about at what level (e.g., individual or institutional) and for which actors segregation is beneficial.

This study is not without limitations. First, the sample is restricted to individuals who survived to age 65, which could bias the estimates if segregation differentially affects survival to 65 by race. Because the death window is truncated, it is not possible to observe early or very late deaths. I provide evidence against this possibility that selection drives these estimates by showing that Gompertz-corrected estimates accounting for double truncation of the death window in the CenSoc Numident data are consistent with regression estimates. 

Second, instrumental variable estimates rely on the exclusion restriction, which is not directly testable. I provide evidence that the railroad divisions are not directly or weakly related to socioeconomic outcomes in 1940 and county-level pollution, which are two leading alternative pathways that plausibly affect longevity. Moreover, I triangulate these estimates using an additional instrument set based on natural waterway boundaries and a sibling fixed effects design, which does not rely on the same exclusion restriction as the instruments. The estimates are consistent across these three strategies in signs and resulting race gaps, which suggests that exclusion restriction violations do not drive these results.

Third, I estimate the effects of segregation in one's death county, after adjusting for birth state and birth year fixed effects to purge the effects of early life context. However, I cannot account for the full exposure history of segregation over the life course nor other important periods which may matter for health. Comparing estimates by migration status derived from birthplace, 1940 residence, and death county offers one partial test of the importance of this heterogeneity. I show that the effect of segregation on longevity is negative and significant across all subgroups; the effects are larger for Black and White stayers and return migrants and smaller for those who migrate permanently (Figure \ref{fig:iv_mig3}). This suggests that migration history across the life course is important for understanding these effects, but does not account for substantial portions of these effects.

% =====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
% =====================================================================
Racial segregation is one of the most significant mechanisms of racial stratification in the United States. The belief that segregation advantages Whites while disadvantaging Blacks is pervasive in scholarship and informs Whites' resistance to integration and policy regarding race in practice \citep{bobo_attitudes_1996,wetts_privilege_2018}. Despite these expectations of White advantage, they are rarely subjected to empirical scrutiny. I show that racial segregation generates Black-White gaps in longevity, but it does so through reductions in the longevity of Black and White Americans at unequal magnitudes. While neighborhood-level mechanisms of segregation have received extensive theoretical attention, these results are inconsistent with a purely neighborhood-level account. I show that political-economic mechanisms of segregation in the form of welfare spending and taxation inequalities, that operate beyond neighborhoods, account for substantial portions of these effects, reinforcing the importance of political context for both racial stratification and health. These findings together suggest that desegregation, rather than disadvantaging Whites, would translate into improvements in longevity for both groups.

\subsection{Monotonicity}

 It is well known that segregation levels have declined over time. If part of that decline reflects less economic and racial residential sorting, then we would expect the association between physical division and segregation to weaken year-over-year. Following \citet{rauscher_why_2020}, evidence for monotonicity would suggest that the instrument strength declines over decades. Figure \ref{fig:iv_monotonicity} displays $R^2$ coefficients from first-stage regressions for the RDI and rivers instruments by death decade. The two instruments behave differently. For the rivers instrument, $R^2$ declines steadily between 1980 and 2000, which is the pattern the monotonicity assumption predicts. For the RDI, $R^2$ falls between 1980 and 1990 and then returns to near its 1980 level by 2000, so its first-stage strength over this period is better described as stable than as declining. While neither pattern constitutes a conclusive test, the declining relationship for the rivers instrument is consistent with the monotonicity assumption, whereas the RDI provides no comparable evidence either for or against it.

\subsection{Birth Cohort and Military Service}

It is possible that military service and coming of age during a period when the U.S. was at war could have selective effects on individuals that could create heterogeneity in these estimates. In this section, I present estimates on birth cohort-stratified models and for a subset of men who served in the military. Examining birth cohorts serves two purposes. The first is to probe how the results vary when considering individuals who were born in earlier versus later historical periods; the second is to provide a partial assessment of the selective impacts of growing up during wartime periods on longevity for the population overall. While no cohorts in my sample are completely untouched by periods of significant wartime combat, examining effects by birth cohort provides a partial assessment of the experiences of individuals who experienced more or less war. That is, those born earlier in the sample were alive during both World War I and World War II, while those born later experienced only World War II. Furthermore, I merged information from the CenSoc Numident Army Enlistment records \citep{breen_censoc_2023} onto these data to examine the heterogeneity of the main estimates for men in the sample who served and did not serve. Appendix Figures \ref{fig:iv_bc-all} and \ref{fig:iv_mil-all} present birth cohort stratified estimates and estimates interacted by military service respectively.

Figure \ref{fig:iv_bc-all} plots RDI IV estimates of the effect of segregation on longevity, expressed as years of life per 10-point rise in dissimilarity, estimated separately within five-year birth-year bins (1905--1909, 1910--1914, 1915--1920) for each race--sex group. Estimates are negative in every cohort and for every group, ranging from roughly $-0.45$ to $-0.68$ years per 10-point rise in $D$. The Rivers IV estimates are considerably noisier than the OLS and RDI IV estimates, but point in the same direction. \subsection{Heterogeneity by Education}
\label{sec:append_ed}
Education is an important axis of social stratification that is causally related to health and may buffer the harmful effects of racial segregation. \citet{dubois_black_1935} argues that the harmful effects of racism are primarily concentrated among lower-class White Americans and Black Americans. To test for this possibility, I present the main segregation estimates interacted with education level in 1940 to examine how the effects of segregation on longevity vary by education. In Appendix Table A14, I present average marginal effects of segregation on longevity for each education category. The final row of Table A14 presents statistical tests of the second differences (i.e., the significance of the difference in the effect between attaining a college degree or higher and attaining less than high school) by method, which Appendix Table A15 reports separately for the two instrumental variable strategies. 

\begin{landscape}
\small \input{../FigTab/IV_education_interaction_only_table.tex}
\end{landscape}

The results indicate that effects of segregation on longevity for Whites are significantly attenuated by about $.070$ to $.186$ years for those with a college degree or higher relative to those with less than a high school education.\footnote{Flexible match sibling fixed effects models are presented for brevity and because they maximize sibling sample size.} These contrasts are significant for the RDI IV but not for the Rivers IV, which could be attributed to the relatively weaker instrument strength of the Rivers IV. Nevertheless, the point estimates for each method are consistent in their direction, albeit variable in magnitude.

Conversely, the effects of segregation on longevity for Blacks do not vary significantly by education level and none of the F-tests for the interaction across any of the identification strategies is significant.

Taken together, and consistent with the predictions of \citet{dubois_black_1935}, these results suggest that Whites who are more advantaged are less harmed by racial segregation, though the effects of segregation are consistently negative even for the most advantaged Whites. Higher-educated Whites are more likely to be advantaged economically and have access to greater resources which could buffer the structural effects of segregation on longevity. Conversely, the effects of segregation on longevity for Black Americans are consistently negative regardless of education level.
